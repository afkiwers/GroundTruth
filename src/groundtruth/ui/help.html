GroundTruth help

GroundTruth checks a KiCad board for return-path problems on its signals, and analyses its switching power stages and motor bridges: hot loops, emission, currents in the copper, losses and temperatures. This page explains what each part of the window does and how the analyses fit together. F1 (or Help in the top bar) opens it at the tab you are on; pressed again, the same help window comes forward at the new tab's section. Links within this page jump to their section; others open in your web browser. The sidebar lists every section and marks the one you are reading; Search (Ctrl+F) shows the sections that mention a word, the word marked (Enter: the next one).

The usual workflow

  1. Open the board. The first time, GroundTruth looks for the nets worth analysing, the DC-DC converters and the motor bridges by itself. What it found shows in orange on the Signal paths, Converters and Bridges tabs until you review it.
  2. Review. Check each row: the driver and receivers of a net, a converter's switches, inductor and operating point, a bridge's legs. Fill in what the board cannot tell: frequencies, edge times, currents, voltages, a motor's data. Press Mark reviewed.
  3. Run checks (seconds): rule checks on the nets' routes against the planes.
  4. Simulate (minutes): the field solver on everything listed. Results go to the Issues and Results tabs.
  5. Look and fix: click an issue to see it in KiCad, look at the current maps, let Suggest fixes (Fixes tab) propose vias and capacitors, try them as what-if edits, place the vias.
  6. Thermal and Current capacity for power stages and bridges, after Simulate.

Every edit on the tabs is saved to the settings file at once (Save settings writes it immediately). The board itself is never changed, except when you place suggested vias or draw on the board.

The top bar, left to right: Reload board reads the board again (after you changed it in KiCad); Refill zones has KiCad refill every zone, waits until it has finished (on a complex board minutes: the status line counts them; KiCad answers nothing meanwhile) and then reloads; Run checks, Simulate, Thermal and Export report… (below); with what-if edits; Cancel stops the running job; Save settings; Clear EMI layers removes what GroundTruth drew. On the right, Preferences… and Help. Refill zones and Clear EMI layers are there only when GroundTruth was started from KiCad's toolbar (Analyse return paths); opened on a board file, it can analyse but not show or draw anything in KiCad. Hover over Thermal, Export report…, with what-if edits, Preferences… or Help for what it does.

The results shown are always of the board and settings they were computed on. When the board was read again and differs (Reload board, or a Simulate that found it changed in KiCad), or a setting the results depend on was changed, the Results tab's summary line starts with OUT OF DATE and says which; Simulate again for the current design. The thermal settings (ambient, convection, limits) are not among them: Thermal reads them when it runs.

Starting GroundTruth. In KiCad's PCB editor the plugin has three actions:

  • Analyse return paths (toolbar button) opens the window on the board open in the PCB editor. A small start window shows at once ("Connecting to KiCad…", "Loading the analysis modules…") and closes when the main window opens. If KiCad cannot be reached or no board is open, a message says so.
  • Add selected net to GroundTruth (toolbar button) adds the net of the track, via or pad selected in the PCB editor to the Signal paths tab (see Signal paths). An open GroundTruth window of the same board takes it and comes to the front (while it runs a Simulate or another job, once that has finished); with none open, a window opens and adds it once it has read the board. With nothing selected that has a net, a message says what KiCad reports as selected.
  • Clear EMI layers (no toolbar button) removes what GroundTruth drew on the board, without opening the window, and says how many items it removed (one undo step).

Without KiCad, groundtruth-gui BOARD.kicad_pcb opens the same window on a board file: everything analyses the same, but the KiCad parts are left out (Refill zones, Clear EMI layers, Add KiCad selection, the zoom and highlight options, the drawing buttons, showing rows and issues in KiCad, the Parts tab following KiCad's selection). Started from KiCad, the window closes by itself when KiCad exits.

When the board is read, the board must have been saved once (its settings go into the groundtruth folder next to it). A settings file with errors is reported and nothing is loaded. When some zones are not filled, GroundTruth asks whether to refill them first. When the settings file is new or lists no nets, converters or bridges, detection runs by itself (a settings file from before the Bridges tab is searched for bridges once). Closing the window saves the settings and clears the net highlight it set in KiCad, giving back your active layer.

The status bar shows what happened last; a long message wraps onto up to three lines (hover over it for all of it). While a job runs it shows what the job is doing, how far it is, the time spent and about how long is left, with a progress bar on the right; a job of 10 s or more says afterwards how long it took. Only one job runs at a time: a button pressed meanwhile is refused with "still working". Cancel stops Run checks, Simulate, Thermal, Current capacity, Suggest fixes, Compare what-if and Export report at their next progress step; reading the board, detection, drawing and Sharper map cannot be cancelled. A settings file that cannot be written is reported there too ("could not save the settings").

What Simulate does with nets, converters and bridges

Everything listed is simulated, every time. Simulate runs every net on the Signal paths tab, every converter on the Converters tab and every motor bridge on the Bridges tab, in one job. To leave something out, remove its row (the detection can find it again later) or keep a second settings file. The command line (groundtruth simulate --nets NAME) can run some of them.

They share the board, not the physics. All of them use one model of the board's copper, read once, and their solves run in parallel. Each one is still solved as if it were alone:

A netA signal from its driver to its receivers at its frequency and edge time. The solver finds where its return current flows in the planes (under the trace, around a slot, through a stitching via or a capacitor, between planes through their capacitance), the loop it makes, and the emission that loop radiates at each harmonic.
A converterIts hot loop: the fast current that moves between the switches and the input capacitors at every edge, at its switching frequency. Also its DC power paths (current density and resistance), its ringing, the conducted emission at its input connector and the radiated estimate of the loop.
A motor bridgeFirst its six-step drive with the motor, at the operating point: the phase, switch and bus currents. Then each leg's hot loop at the leg's largest switched current, all legs on one model. Together: the conducted emission of all legs at the battery input, the bus capacitors' ripple, the RMS current density from the battery to the motor, the parts' losses, and the sweep over other currents and speeds.

No switching current is fed into the nets' solves. A net on the Signal paths tab shows its own return path, not the noise a converter or a bridge puts on it. That interaction has its own analysis:

  • Victims of a bridge or a converter. When a bridge or a power stage is simulated, GroundTruth finds the microcontroller, reset, crystal, Hall, debug and PWM / gate-input nets on the board by itself (or takes the ones listed under victims: in the settings), puts them into the stage's model and computes the noise each one's receiver sees at the switching edges. This does not depend on the Signal paths tab. The results are in the bridge's drive view, in each stage's part of the report, and as victim_coupling issues.

Where the results come together:

  • Issues lists every problem of every net, converter and bridge, sorted by severity.
  • Results has a view per net route, converter, DC path, bridge, bridge leg, RMS map and sweep.
  • Thermal and Current capacity add the heat of every converter and every bridge on the board: they share the copper.
  • Suggest fixes works through all the issues and proposes fixes per net, converter or bridge leg; a fix is checked on every net it could disturb.

Run checks and Simulate

Run checks follows each listed net from its driver to its receivers (through series resistors and ferrites) and checks every segment against the planes next to it: crossing a gap or a split, running off a plane or along its edge, changing layer without a stitching via (same reference net) or a capacitor (different nets), referencing a non-ground plane. It also runs the board-wide voltage-aware safety checks (spacing for the voltage, capacitor ratings, ESD parts at connectors). It takes seconds.

Simulate runs the checks and then the field solver on everything listed (see above). The status line shows what it is doing, how far it is and the time left; Cancel stops it. With with what-if edits ticked, both run on a copy of the board with the enabled what-if edits (virtual vias, capacitors, fills) added; with none enabled, it says where to add them.

Run checks without nets runs the board-wide checks only; Simulate needs a net, a converter, a bridge, a DC load or a PDN rail. Afterwards the status line counts the errors, warnings and infos (waived ones left out) and the window turns to the Issues tab (Run checks) or the Results tab (Simulate).

The settings are checked first. A value that looks like a missing unit is refused with what to write instead (a C_oss of 482 is 482 F: write 482p); a bridge without its operating point or motor data is listed with what is missing.

Signal paths tab

The signal paths to analyse, one row each: a path runs from its driver pin to its receiver pins, across nets through series parts (0R, termination resistors, ferrites), the parts named in Pass through and op-amp stages, and is solved as one. Each row has its frequency, edge time and amplitude (optional: source and load, current). A path on one net is simply a path without parts.

The table's columns: Path (named after its start net), Status, Chain (the path as the route follows it: U302.3 → U301 ×1 → R303 4k7 → U1102.9, op-amps with their gain; worked out in the background, "no route: …" when the copper or a part doesn't connect), Freq, Rise, Ampl. and Class (the kind of signal detection took it for). Under the table, the selected path in full: its chain and the parts across it to ground or a rail (a load resistor, a filter capacitor), which take their share of the current in Simulate. Status is manual (entered by you), auto, REVIEW (found by detection, not checked yet: the row is orange) or auto, reviewed.

Where Detect misses a path, takes it for a supply, guesses its driver wrong or stops at an op-amp, label the path in the schematic: see Labelling a signal path.

  • The form: Start net (pick it from the list of the board's nets or type it); then Driver pin and Receiver pins list the pins of that net (also across series parts) to choose from; Frequency, Rise time, Amplitude (V) and Duty cycle (0.5 for a clock). Values take units and SI prefixes (20MHz, 2ns, 3.3).
  • A signal path across nets: the driver and receiver pins may be on different nets joined by series parts (a damping resistor, a ferrite, an inductor): the path runs through them and each part's impedance (from the Parts tab) counts, at its place along the path. A part that doesn't pass a signal by default (an AC-coupling capacitor, a resistor above 1 kΩ) is passed when named in Pass through (e.g. C12); when no path is found, the issue names the parts in the way. The source: Source R (Ω), Source L (nH, package and bond wire) and Source C (pF, its output capacitance), Fall time when it differs from the rise time. The load: Load C (pF per receiver) and Load R (Ω, a termination at the receivers). Empty fields use the defaults (25 Ω, 5 pF, no L, C or termination). The report shows the path per net: its stretches and parts with their |Z| at the worst harmonic. Parts from the path to ground or a rail (a termination, an RC or pi filter's capacitor, a pull-down) are found on their own: each takes its share of the current at its place, and one that carries at least 1 % of it is a current loop of its own (the return flows back through the plane from its ground pin), solved with the net. The report lists them with their current; the notes name the loops. An op-amp on the path (part model amplifier, see Parts tab) splits it into stages: the driver drives the op-amp's input, the op-amp drives the rest with its gain (rolled off at its bandwidth or GBW / gain, capped by its slew rate) through its output impedance, and each side's current returns at the op-amp's ground pin: two current loops. The maps then show the output side's.
  • Detect… suggests nets from their names and the schematic's pin types (SPI, RMII, USB, SDIO, debug clocks, crystals …), with the driver from the pin types and the series parts followed, and default timing to review. A dialog lists what it found that is not listed yet (with its class, driver, receivers, timing, its Confidence and Why); the likely ones are ticked. OK adds the ticked nets as unreviewed. A net already listed, still unreviewed and detected, whose path detection now sees differently (after GT labels were added, say) is offered again, ticked and marked REPLACES: it replaces the entry (its tick kept), and unreviewed entries for pieces of its path are removed. Reviewed and hand-made nets are never changed.
  • A whole path from KiCad: select one bit of copper on each net of the path in the PCB editor (a track, via or pad per net, Ctrl+click to add, any order), then Add KiCad selection or the toolbar button. The nets are put in order by the parts that join them; an op-amp's input and output pins give the direction (else the pin types at the ends; a selected pad fixes that end's pin). One row is added: driver → series parts and op-amps → receivers, the resistors that pass no signal by default in Pass through, the op-amps modelled as amplifiers with their two pins (Parts tab). The GT labels this means for the schematic symbols (sig_drv, amp_in / amp_out, sig_rx, see labels) are written into the open schematic where KiCad allows it. That needs KiCad 11 (see the note): on KiCad 10 they are shown in a message to add by hand (Symbol Fields Table, a GT column; Ctrl+C copies the message). The path works in Simulate right away either way. Selected nets no part joins are added one by one.
  • Add KiCad selection (or KiCad's toolbar button Add selected net to GroundTruth) adds the net of the track, via or pad selected in the PCB editor (click or right-click it there first), as an unreviewed suggestion with its driver and receivers from the pin types; the status line says what was added, and asks for the frequency and rise time where the net's kind of signal is unknown. A net of unknown kind driven by an op-amp with a Slew Rate or GBW / Bandwidth field (an analog output, a buffered sensor) gets the fastest full swing that op-amp can make instead of 10 MHz / 2 ns: a TLV6001 (0.5 V/µs) gives 5.28 µs edges at 48.2 kHz. Set the real signal if it is slower. Ground nets and power-stage switch nodes are refused with the reason. A net that looks like a supply (a power pin on it, a supply name such as +3V3, VIN, RAIL) or carries a power stage's current is refused too, but you are asked whether to add it as a signal anyway: an ADC input or a sense line measuring a rail is one. A name with a measuring word (ADC, SENSE, SNS, MEAS, MON, FB) is taken as a signal without asking (/CPU/ADC_I_RAIL_1). A net already listed is selected.
  • The tick box at the start of a row switches the net on or off. Switched off, it keeps everything you entered (in the settings file as enabled: false) and shows greyed, but Run checks, Simulate and the fix search leave it out, and it is not checked against the board. The Converters and Bridges tabs have the same tick box. With every row off, Simulate says so. The command line runs a switched-off row only when --nets names it.
  • The form on the right edits the selected row: Add (a new net from the form), Update selected (one row only; editing a detected net counts as reviewing it), Remove selected, Mark reviewed (the orange stops). Hover over a field or a button for what it means. With no nets yet, a line above the table says how to start.
  • Clicking a row fills the form and highlights the net's whole path in KiCad, from its driver through its series parts, Pass through parts and op-amps to its receivers (Zoom KiCad to the selected nets also zooms to it; ~ in KiCad clears the highlight). Several rows can be selected (Ctrl / Shift-click, Ctrl+A), removed (Delete) or marked reviewed at once.
  • How nets are found at the bottom, Show / Hide: the net and pin names detection looks for (the text can be selected and copied).

Converters tab

The switching power stages (DC-DC converters): name, topology, rail, switch node, ground, the switches (high side, low side, or an IC with its switches inside), switching frequency, input and output voltage, load current, edge time, and optionally C_oss / Q_oss, R_DS(on), the inductor's DCR, a diode's V_f, the stray capacitance to a chassis, the input and output connectors and the conducted standard. Hover over a field for what it means. C_oss, Q_oss, R_DS(on), DCR and V_f come from the parts' schematic fields where they have them: ◆ beside a field, or ! where a value set here differs; Use schematic values takes them back (Part parameters from the schematic). The controller's spread spectrum is on the form: Spread spectrum (%) (f_sw dithered up to that much above it; empty: off), Spread mode (random hops, or a triangle sweep) and Spread rate (the triangle's modulation frequency, or random hops per second; empty: every cycle). Interleave group and Phase (°) mark the phases of one controller (Interleaved stages below). The buttons are those of the Signal paths tab, and the rows show in KiCad the same way: clicking a row selects the stage's switches and hot-loop capacitors and highlights its switch node (Zoom KiCad to the selected power stages also zooms). KiCad shows the row you stop on: clicking or arrowing quickly through the rows does not make it select and zoom to each one.

The table's columns: Name, Status (as on the Signal paths tab), Topology, Switches (high side / low side, or "integrated" and the IC), Rail, f_sw, I out (the load current: the output's; the input current is worked out, see below), V in→out (? where not set), Edge, Input and Output (the connector pins). The form scrolls when the window is short. In the form, Topology is buck, boost or custom; Integrated stage takes an IC with both switches inside (leave High side, Low side and Switch node empty then); Conducted standard (analysis default) uses the settings' analysis.conducted; parts are references separated by commas (Q1, Q2), connectors plus then minus (J1.1, J1.2). R_DS(on), R_DS(on) low side, Inductor DCR and Diode V_f (V) are the losses of the thermal solve.

Dead time, reverse conduction and recovery (a synchronous stage: a FET as the rectifier, a buck's low side or a boost's high side). Before each switch turns on, both are off for a dead time; the inductor current meanwhile flows through the rectifier FET in reverse, at Reverse V_SD (about 1.5-2 V for GaN, 0.8 V for a silicon body diode) instead of across its R_DS(on). Each edge of the switch node has its own: Dead time, rise (low side off → high side on; the LTC7890's DTCA) and Dead time, fall (high side off → low side on; its DTCB). At the main switch's turn-on (a buck's rise, the hard edge) the whole dead time is reverse conduction, at the ripple's valley current. At its turn-off (a buck's fall, the soft edge) the inductor current, at its peak, first swings the node across, moving both FETs' output charge (2·Q_oss, else 2·C_oss·V): only the rest of that dead time is reverse conduction. The rectifier's channel is off for both. With GaN the loss is often as much as the FET's conduction (kraft_flux_mini_2's conv1 at 10 A with 2 × 20 ns: 0.25 W, against 0.15 W of conduction; the fall's 7 ns node swing takes 0.07 W off). The dead time is not the edge time: the edge is how fast the node swings once a switch turns on. Set them here, or give the controller IC a GT field (Detection labels): dead:20ns for both edges of every converter it controls, dead1:20ns / dead2:30ns per channel (its SW1 / SW2 pin), conv1_dead:20ns for the converter labelled conv1, and any of them for one edge: dead_rise:20ns, dead1_fall:10ns, conv1_dead_rise:15ns (for each edge the most specific word wins). The values are then marked ◆ like the parts' fields. Empty: 20 ns (an adaptive controller's; the LTC7890 with its DTCA / DTCB pins on GND). V_SD comes from the rectifier FETs' Vsd field, else 2 V for a GaN part and 0.8 V otherwise. A silicon FET's body diode also has a reverse-recovery charge Q_rr (its Qrr field; GaN has none, leave it empty): the main switch sweeps it out at its hard turn-on (Q_rr·V·f_sw of loss), the hot loop carries it as a current spike of I_rr = √(2·Q_rr·di/dt) (in the conducted and radiated spectra), and when the recovery snaps off it adds an overshoot I_rr·√(L/C_oss), shown on the Results tab's recovery tile and in the hot_loop issue. Thermal's notes say when a default was used.

  • Detect… finds stages from their inductors and switches; a surge stopper or reverse-polarity FET in the input path is recognised and taken as closed (give it a resistor model of its R_DS(on) on the Parts tab). A dialog lists the stages found (switches, rail, ground, connectors, confidence, why); the likely ones are ticked, OK adds them as unreviewed: set the switching frequency, load current, voltages and edge time.
  • What Simulate gives per stage: the hot-loop inductance and L·di/dt overshoot, the ringing with C_oss, which capacitors carry the loop current, the radiated and the conducted estimate (through a LISN at the input connector), a common-mode budget, input-filter guidance, and the DC power paths with their current density and via loads.
  • Interleaved stages. The channels of a dual or multiphase controller (two bucks driven by one LTC7890, say) switch at one frequency, each delayed by its phase. Give them the same Interleave group name and each its Phase (°) from the controller's datasheet (empty: the group's stages 360°/n apart, in list order). Detect… proposes a group for the stages one IC switches, 0° and 180° for two: check the datasheet, some controllers run their channels in phase. Simulate then adds the stages' input currents at the LISN and their hot loops' fields at the antenna as phasors, per harmonic of f_sw: 180° apart they cancel at the odd harmonics (all of it where the two stages are alike, at the fundamental most of all) and add at the even ones. Each member stage shows the group's conducted and radiated margin together, the same sum in phase for comparison, and each stage's own margin. A group below the EMI margin has its own issue (interleave group …); the stages' own issues stay, they are what each stage would do alone. The group needs its stages at one switching frequency and the same spread spectrum (else it is not combined, a note says why); MIL-STD-461 CE101 is combined the same way for stages switching within its range (the limit at their input current together). DC and thermal results need nothing: each switch node's current stays in its own copper.
  • DC paths on the whole board, and all stages together. Every stage's DC power paths (switch node, input, output, ground return) are solved on one copper model of the whole board, so nothing is cut off at the edge of a box around the stage and the current spreads as far as the copper takes it. With two or more stages the Results tab's Route list starts with all power stages DC currents together: every stage at its own load at once, their currents added (they share the input connector, a surge stopper, the rail and the ground). Its title gives the loads, the total input current and the copper's loss of the total current. The current-density and via-load checks run on this total, as issues of all power stages; the stages' own DC views still show what each stage alone puts where. The switch node carries the current from the high side for the duty D and from the low side for the rest: its densities, via currents and copper loss are the RMS of the two (the arrows show the mean flow), and Thermal heats the copper with that.
  • The input current is not entered: it follows from the energy balance V_in·I_in = V_out·I_out + losses (a buck at 100 % efficiency would draw I_out·D). The losses are the ones Thermal uses: the switches' conduction (R_DS(on)), the hard edge (½·V·I·t_r·f_sw) and output charge (Q_oss·V·f_sw), a diode's V_f, the inductor's DCR, series parts and the copper of the DC paths; gate drive and the controller's own supply are not counted, and a value not given (no R_DS(on), no DCR) counts as no loss: the stage's notes say which. The stage's line on the Results tab shows the input current and efficiency, its notes the loss break-down, and the DC input path's map (connector to the high side) carries this current: its title reads "expected input current … A (efficiency … %)" beside the path's resistance and voltage drop. The ground return splits by it too: the input connector's minus pin takes the input current back, the low side the rest.
  • The radiated estimate counts the charge each edge moves into the switch node (C_oss, or Q_oss where given) on top of the load current: near the ringing it can be several times the load pulse (a light load on a high input voltage). Without C_oss there is neither ringing nor that current: for a diode buck give the diode's junction capacitance as C_oss. The estimate is the hot loop's own radiation; cables usually radiate more, so read it as a lower bound.
  • Ceramic input capacitors lose much of their capacitance at a high DC voltage (an X7R keeps roughly 30-60 % at half its rating). A stage's notes name those still at their nominal value: give their C at bias on the Parts tab.
  • The line at the bottom (Labels (GT field)) says which detection labels the board carries; Show explains them (see Detection labels). The Bridges tab has the same line.

Bridges tab

Motor bridges: half-bridge legs on one bus, the motor's winding as the inductor (BLDC drives, ESCs, starter-generators). The legs, their phase nodes, drivers, snubbers and the motor connector are detected; the operating point and the motor are yours to enter:

Operating pointbus voltage, PWM frequency, phase current (the mean current of the conducting phases), mode (motoring or generating), speed (0 = locked rotor, cranking).
PWM schemehpwm_lon: the sourcing phase's high side chops, the sinking phase's low side stays on; hon_lpwm: the mirror; bipolar: both conducting legs chop. Synchronous: the chopping leg's other switch conducts in the off time (complementary PWM with dead time). A scope on a leg's two gates shows which.
Switchesdead time (between one gate turning off and the other turning on), edge time, R_DS(on) of one FET, reverse-conduction voltage, C_oss and Q_oss of one side's parallel FETs together. The FETs' schematic fields fill these where they have them, marked as on the Converters tab (Part parameters from the schematic).
Motorstar or delta; resistance and inductance between two motor leads; back-EMF constant as the line-to-line peak per 1000 rpm (from a datasheet's V AC RMS per 1000 rpm: × 1.414; from Kv in rpm/V: about 1000 / Kv); pole pairs (poles / 2); trapezoidal or sinusoidal EMF.
Sweepphase currents and speeds that Simulate also evaluates, every current at every speed, from the same solves (a few seconds each).

The form also has the bridge's Name, Bus net, Ground net, Input connector (+ and - pins, where the LISN goes), Conducted standard and C stray (chassis), and under Motor Advance (°): the commutation advance in electrical degrees (negative: retarded). Hover over a field for what it means and an example value.

The table's columns: Name, Status, Legs (how many), Bus, V, f_pwm, I phase, rpm, Motor and Input. A bridge stays orange while it is unreviewed or while Simulate still lacks its operating point or motor data: its Status then reads auto, SET UP or set operating point.

The legs of the bridge in the form are listed under the bridges (Legs of the bridge in the form: phase, switch node, high side, low side, output pin, driver and the nets it is driven from). Clicking a leg fills the Selected leg fields (Phase, Switch node, High side, Low side, Output pin); Set leg replaces the selected leg with them, Add leg adds one, Remove leg removes the selected one. A leg needs its switch node, high side and low side. Leg edits belong to the form: Update selected (or Add) stores them with the bridge. Clicking a bridge shows it in KiCad like a converter (Zoom KiCad to the selected bridges). The other buttons are those of the Signal paths tab; Detect… shows the bridges found (legs, bus, input, confidence, why) with the complete, likely ones ticked.

Simulate then, per bridge:

  • drives the motor in six-step at the operating point and finds the duty for the phase current (an issue says when the bus cannot drive it at that speed);
  • solves each leg's hot loop: inductance, L·di/dt, ringing (with the leg's snubber), radiated estimate. A leg that does not switch at the operating point (a locked rotor drives one commutation step) gets no hot-loop checks;
  • adds the legs at the input: the conducted emission at the battery, read by a simulated EMI receiver against the limit;
  • shares the ripple among the bus capacitors;
  • maps the RMS currents from the battery to the motor (phase currents, bus current, switching currents) with the busiest vias against their IPC-2221 rating;
  • counts the parts' losses: each FET's conduction, reverse conduction in the dead times, hard turn-on and output charge; the snubber resistors; the capacitors' ESR; the series parts on the battery path (a part modelled as a short gets no loss: give it a resistor model);
  • checks the victims (see above) and runs the sweep.

On the Results tab a bridge has four kinds of view: its drive (phase and bus currents, capacitor ripple, conducted spectrum, losses), each leg's hot loop, its RMS currents map, and its sweep (losses, RMS density, conducted and radiated margin against the phase current, one line per speed; a hollow marker where the current is not reached).

DC loads tab

A DC load is a supply rail with the currents its loads draw: a power connector feeding several boards' connectors, a pour or track to a regulator, a fused output. Simulate solves each rail on the copper of the whole board (pours, tracks and vias, as for a power stage's DC paths) and shows:

  • the voltage drop each load sees: on the supply side (from the source pin to the load's pin) and, when the return pins are given, on the ground side (back to the source's return pin), and the total;
  • the current density with its flow (a view of its own on the Results tab), the hottest narrow spot and the busiest vias against IPC-2221, and the copper's loss; these are checked like a power stage's DC paths (current_density, via_overload), and a load above the rail's Max drop is an issue (dc_drop). The checked density is the one sustained over 1 mm of the rail's own copper (another net's pour beside a track does not dilute it); its limits are rules of thumb, and what a density does to the copper's temperature is Thermal's. The peak shown beside it is the density over a quarter millimetre of copper (or one cell, where the cell is larger): at an inside corner the density has no finite value at a point, so a single cell's figure would grow on every finer grid; over 0.25 mm it settles (a coarse grid reads it up to about 10 % low);
  • Thermal heats the board with it, and Current capacity finds the current at which the hottest copper or part reaches the limit, all the rail's loads scaled together.

Each load draws its current whatever the drop (a current sink), and the source pin is the reference (an ideal source). So narrowing a track to a quarter of its width gives that track four times the resistance, drop, loss and current density at the same current. The drops are for copper at 20 °C: about 0.4 % more per kelvin the board runs warmer; after Thermal, its notes (and the JSON report's dc_at_temperature) give each DC path's resistance, drop and loss at the temperature the solve found along it. Copper of the rail's net that does not touch (a track broken by a gap) is not joined, however narrow the gap: the rail is then reported as not connected.

Fill from net… is the quick way: pick the rail's net (e.g. VDD) and its ground (GND); GroundTruth lists every part with a pin on the rail, with that part's own ground pin, and you pick the part feeding it (the connector with the fewest pins is preselected) and the parts drawing from it (the connectors are preselected), then a current per load. The form is filled in; check it, change a line's current if a load draws more, and press Add.

Typing pins: the Source pin, Source return and Loads fields list the board's pins while you type, each with its net and part (Ctrl+Space, or Down in a one-line field, lists them on an empty field); Up/Down choose, Enter or Tab takes one, Escape closes the list. Typing matches a pin's start (j3: J3's pins) or, from two letters, its net (vdd). The order follows the field: the source offers supply connectors first, the return the source part's own ground pin first; in Loads, a line's pin comes from the source pin's net (pins already listed are left out), followed by a "/" and its own part's pin on the return's net, then "=" for the current.

The form: Name; Source pin, the pin the rail is fed from (REF.PAD, e.g. J1.2); Source return, optional (e.g. J1.1, GND); Loads, one per line as PIN/RETURN=CURRENT, e.g. J2.2/J2.1=0.5A (without returns: J2.2=0.5A; give the returns for every load and the source, or for none); Max drop, optional, in volts. Every load draws its current at once (the solve is linear: one load alone is not needed to know its share). The tick box at the start of a row switches it off without losing it. With power stages on the same copper, the DC currents together view adds the rails too. The model's cells start at the board's dielectric height: a pour reads a few % low at 1x resolution (0.24 mm cells widen its edges); 4x is converged.

In the settings file:

dc_loads:
  - {name: VDD rail, source: J1.2, source_return: J1.1, max_drop: 200mV, loads: [{pin: J2.2, return: J2.1, current: 500mA}, {pin: J3.2, return: J3.1, current: 500mA}]}

Power integrity tab (PDN impedance)

For a supply rail, the impedance its ICs see between their supply and ground pins, from 10 kHz to 1 GHz: the decaps (their C, ESR and ESL from the Parts tab, plus their pads and vias), the planes' capacitance, the copper and the regulator together, solved on one model of the whole board. Each IC with supply pins (schematic pin type power_in) on the rail and a pad on its ground is a port: a current step at its pins and the voltage it makes there. The rail holds its voltage within the allowed ripple when the impedance stays below the target V · ripple / I_step, whatever the frequency of the IC's current step.

  • per IC the curve |Z(f)| with its phase below it (−90°: capacitive, +90°: inductive, crossing 0° at a resonance; the HTML report's Power integrity part and the rail's view), its peaks (anti-resonances: two decaps, or the decaps and the planes, ringing together), the part holding the rail at each frequency (the regulator, a decap, the planes) and the inductance above its decaps;
  • issues: pdn_impedance where an IC's curve is over the target below check_to (default 100 MHz: above it the package and the die's own capacitance take over), naming the part holding the rail there; decap_missing for supply pins with no decap pad within decap_mm (3 mm);
  • the source (the regulator's output, found by its power_out pin, or a connector) is modelled as vrm_r + jωvrm_l (5 mΩ, 20 nH unless set): below the decaps' range the rail is the regulator's.

The model is the whole board at the board-wide resolution (Preferences), and each IC's pins with their nearby decaps on a fine model of their own: the inductance at small pads and vias, which the board-wide grid cannot resolve, is added to the IC's curve. An IC whose small pads the board-wide grid cannot join to the rail's copper is left out with a note (raise the resolution).

The Power integrity tab lists the rails: Find rails adds every rail ICs draw supply from (its source found by itself); set each one's Load step for a target (and its voltage when the name has none). After Simulate, each rail is a view on the Results tab: the curves with the target, and under them what holds the rail at each IC across frequency. In the settings file:

pdn:
  - {rail: +3V3, i_step: 300mA}
  - {rail: VDD_CORE, voltage: 1.2V, ripple: 3%, i_step: 2A, source: U7, vrm_r: 2mΩ, vrm_l: 10nH}

Signal integrity tab

The characteristic impedance of the listed nets along their tracks, differential pairs, crosstalk and via stubs, from cross-sections of the board: a cut across every copper layer perpendicular to the track, every 2 mm, with all the copper in it (other nets, pours and planes as the reference at 0 V), the stackup's dielectrics and the solder mask, solved as a two-dimensional field problem (within 0.2 % of exact results). Run checks does it: a few seconds per net; the figures show on this tab and in the report, the issues on the Issues tab.

  • impedance: Z0 along a net's tracks outside its target. Targets on this tab (Impedance target: nets by name or pattern such as /CPU/SPI_*, or a KiCad net class, with Z0 and tolerance). A listed net without a target gets its Z0 shown. A void in the plane under a track shows as a higher Z0 there.
  • diff_impedance, diff_skew: a pair's differential impedance where its two nets run side by side (elsewhere it is uncoupled, about twice Z0), and the delay mismatch of their routed lengths against Max skew (by default a tenth of the pair's edge). Find pairs lists the pairs found by name (_P/_N, +/-, CAN_H/CAN_L, DP/DM, X and X*), with the interface's impedance where the name tells it (USB 90 Ω, Ethernet, LVDS, HDMI 100 Ω); CAN and RS-485 get none (their slow edges need none on the board).
  • crosstalk: a listed net (its edge and swing from the Signal paths tab) running beside another track on one layer: near-end and far-end crosstalk as a share of its swing, from the coupled length, the spacing and the edge; above the limit (5 %) an issue on the victim.
  • KiCad tuning profiles (Board Setup > Tuning Profiles, assigned to a net class): the profile's target impedance is its nets' target (a Differential profile's, its pairs'), and tuning_delay compares its unit delay per layer with the cross-sections' when it tunes in time: KiCad turns lengths into delays with the figure typed there (FR-4 microstrip is about 60 ps/cm). A profile on the Default class applies to the listed nets.
  • via_stub: a via of a listed net or pair whose barrel runs on past the layers it uses: the stub resonates at a quarter wave; below 3 × the signal's knee (0.35 / rise time) an issue (back-drill, a blind via, or change layers near the via's end).
si:
  impedance:
    - {nets: [/CPU/SPI_*], z0: 50Ω, tol: 10%}
    - {class: HighSpeed, z0: 45Ω}
  pairs:
    - {p: /USB/D_P, n: /USB/D_N, zdiff: 90Ω, rise_time: 500ps, max_skew: 10ps}
  crosstalk_max: 5%
  stub_factor: 3
  step_mm: 2

Voltage-aware safety checks

What each net's voltage asks of the copper and the parts around it. A net's voltage comes from its name (+12V, VDD_3V3), the grounds and reference nets (0 V), a converter's input rail and switch node (its input voltage; a boost's output) and its output after the inductor, a motor bridge's bus and phases, a PDN rail, and through parts in series with (nearly) no drop: 0R, shunts and resistors up to 10 Ω, inductors, ferrites, and a pass FET given a short or resistor model on the Parts tab (its drain and source, not its gate). A voltage of your own wins (settings safety: voltages).

  • clearance_voltage (Run checks): copper of two nets on one layer closer than IPC-2221B Table 6-1 asks for their voltage difference, at the closest point: B1 on inner layers, B2 on outer layers, B4 when the board is coated (safety: coated: true); only above 30 V (below it the fab's basic spacing applies). An error; a warning when the other net's voltage is not known and is taken as 0 V (give it under safety: voltages). Voltages are ranges: a switch node swings from 0 V to its rail; a FET's gate rides on its source and a bootstrap net on its switch node, up to the gate drive (gate_drive_v, 10 V) above it, so a bootstrap net and its switch node are only that far apart. A part's own pads against each other are not checked (its maker rates them); a track near them is.
  • cap_voltage (Run checks): a capacitor's voltage against its rating (its Rated Voltage or Voltage field, else "50V" in its value), derated by kind (ceramic and tantalum 50 %, aluminium 80 %): over the rating an error, over the derated value a warning; capacitors at 10 V or more without a rating are listed in one info issue.
  • esd_protection (Run checks): a connector's pins with no ESD / TVS part on their net within 10 mm (esd_mm): info, or a warning for the connectors listed in esd_connectors (the board's outside world).
  • resistor_power (Simulate): a resistor's loss I²R in the DC paths or a motor bridge's RMS current against its Rated Power or Power field (1/16W as written; else by its package: 0402 1/16 W … 2512 1 W), above 50 % of it a warning; resistors dissipating 50 mW or more without a rating are listed in one info issue.
  • vds_rating (Simulate): a power stage's switch-node peak (its rail plus the ringing's overshoot) against the switches' Vds field, above 80 % of it a warning.
safety:
  voltages: {V_BATT: 58V}
  coated: false
  cap_derating: {ceramic: 50%}
  resistor_derating: 50%
  vds_derating: 80%
  esd_connectors: [J1, J4]

Parts tab

Every part on the board with the electrical model the solver uses for it: short, open, resistor (R), cap (C, ESR, ESL, and optionally C at bias), inductor (L, R), ferrite (R, L), amplifier (an op-amp stage: gain, and optionally GBW, bandwidth, output impedance, input C and R, slew rate, CMRR). Models are suggested from the value and footprint, and from the symbol's fields where it has them (Part parameters from the schematic); correct them where it matters:

  • an op-amp in a signal path: give it the amplifier model (a Gain field on its symbol does it by itself) and, for a dual or quad op-amp, its stage's input and output pins (pins, or the GT labels amp_in:3, amp_out:1). The path runs through it: see Signal paths tab.
  • a signal route runs through a part whose model passes it (0R, a termination resistor up to 1 kΩ, a ferrite);
  • capacitors, 0R and ferrites between plane nets are branches between the planes (decoupling, bridges across splits): their C and ESL matter for the return paths and the hot loops;
  • a current sensor, an ideal-diode or surge-stopper FET in a power path is detected as a short: give it a resistor model with its real resistance for the DC paths and the losses;
  • a ceramic capacitor on a high voltage: C at bias is its capacitance at its DC voltage, read from the maker's DC-bias curve (a 10 µF X7R rated 100 V keeps roughly 3-6 µF at 50 V). Where it is given, the solver uses it instead of C; empty, the nominal C.

The table's columns: Ref, Value, Footprint (read only), Model (a drop-down), R (Ω), C (F), C at bias (F), L (H), ESR (Ω), ESL (H), Pins (a through-hole part's own pin values, see below), Schematic and Note. Only the parameters of the part's model can be edited; the others are shaded. A Model cell is coloured when its model still lacks a parameter it needs. An emptied parameter cell removes that value; text that is not a number is refused and the old value comes back. Every edit is saved to the settings at once.

Filter narrows the list by reference, value or footprint (or a reference prefix such as C); Set filtered parts to a model and Apply changes all the listed parts at once (after asking), Reset filtered to suggested takes them back to the suggested models. Values accept SI notation (33R, 4k7, 100n). An open part is only ports; a short is a dead short. Clicking a part in KiCad selects its row while this tab shows (a filter that hides it is cleared) and puts the cursor on its Model cell, ready to edit.

Through-hole pins (a connector, a big inductor's leads): how a part's pins conduct through the board and join it. Built in, a soldered pin is copper filling the whole drilled hole, joined to every layer around its ring, with nothing in series: a perfect joint. Where that is too kind, give the part its values, comma separated, e.g. contact:0.5m, fill:75, pin:brass:

  • contact: the mating contact's resistance per pin (the connector's datasheet, e.g. 0.5m = 0.5 mΩ);
  • joint: the solder joint's resistance per pin;
  • fill: how far up the hole the solder goes, in % of the board's thickness from the soldering side (the side opposite the part). 100 built in; IPC-A-610 accepts 75. Above the solder only the hole's thin plating joins the layers to the pin, and the part's current enters the board where the solder ends;
  • pin: the pin's conductor: hole (built in: copper filling the hole), copper, brass, bronze (phosphor bronze), becu or tellurium. With a material the pin is a rod of pin_d (mm; default the hole less 0.25 mm), the gap is solder and the plating conducts alongside; pin_r gives the pin's own resistance through the board instead.

Each value comes from the first place that has it: the part's GT field in the schematic (beside its labels: GT = conv_in, contact:0.5m), else its Pins cell on this tab, else Pin defaults… (the board's values, for every part without its own), else built in. The Schematic column shows a GT field's values (◆ GT …). contact and joint sit in series where a part's current enters the board (a DC load's source and load pins, a converter's input and output connectors): they add I·R to a DC load's drop (the Loads table's "of which at pins") and I²·R of heat at the part in Thermal; the current in the copper does not change. No contact value is guessed: a connector pin carrying 3 A or more without one gets a note. fill and pin change the hole's conduction between layers in every analysis.

Part parameters from the schematic (InvenTree fields)

R_DS(on), C_oss, an inductor's DCR, a capacitor's ESR and the like can come from the parts' symbol fields instead of being typed in here: give the parts these fields in the schematic, or the parameter templates in InvenTree (KiCad's HTTP library hands them to the symbols as fields). Each parameter has one field name, matched without regard to case. A value with a unit is read as InvenTree sends it (3.8milliohm, 616 picofarad, 38 nanocoulomb, 6.9 mΩ) or in datasheet notation (3.8m, 616p, 4m7); a bare number is in the unit the table names. A value of the wrong kind (100V for Rds(on)) is not used, and the stage's or part's note says why. A value outside the usual range (the table's column) is your value: it is used as written, and a power stage's is listed on the Issues tab (unusual_value, info) in case a unit is missing.

Issues tab

Every problem found, by severity (error, warning, info). Clicking one shows it in KiCad: the copper concerned is selected, its net highlighted and the view zoomed to it (nothing in the design changes); Zoom to it and Highlight its net switch those off, Show in KiCad shows the selected issue again after you moved KiCad's view. Draw markers on board puts numbered markers on a drawing layer. (These need GroundTruth started from KiCad.) A finding you accept can be waived with Waive selected… (with a reason, kept in the settings file). Issue kinds include gap crossings, missing stitching vias or capacitors at layer changes, return detours, plane resonances, emission margins, hot-loop overshoot, conducted emission, current density, via overload, victim coupling and temperatures. band_unsolved says that a solve did not converge: above the last solved frequency the emission is only held at its value there, so a resonance in that band would be missed; Simulate again at another resolution (Preferences). The worst emission is always a solved frequency, not one read between solves (if it cannot be, a note says so); when the solve budget (analysis.max_solves) ends before every gap near the limit is resolved, a note names where a resonance could still hide: raise the budget there. A dc_unsolved warning names a DC path (or a motor bridge's leg) whose solve failed: the stage's loss, input current and Thermal's heat leave it out. A connector's or other part's soldered pin is not checked as a via: the pin carries the current between the layers.

  • The columns: #, Severity, Check (the kind of issue), Net, Location (x, y in mm), Layer and Message. Rows are coloured by severity; a waived issue is greyed, reads "(waived)" and is not counted in the status line's totals.
  • The line under the list gives the selected issue's title and its fingerprint (what a waiver is tied to).
  • Double-click or Enter on a row shows it in KiCad again, like Show in KiCad. ~ in KiCad clears the net highlight. The status line says what was shown, or that its copper is no longer on the board (then Reload board and run the checks again).
  • Waive selected… asks for the reason; an empty reason waives nothing.
  • Checks… chooses which checks the list shows (with how many issues each has); the others are hidden, not waived, and Draw markers on board leaves them out too. Every issue keeps its number, so a row and its marker on the board still match. The choice is kept on this computer; the line beside the button says how many are hidden.
  • Opened on a board file, clicking an issue tells in the status line what it is about and where.

Results tab

One view per analysed item: pick it in the Route list at the top, and the copper layer in Layer. Current arrows, Log scale and Cross-section switch those parts of the view on and off and keep their state for next time. How vias and parts are marked is set in Preferences.

  • Route lists a signal route as its net and driver → receiver, a converter's DC path as "DC" and the path's name, a bridge's RMS map as "RMS currents", and the bridge, sweep and thermal views by name. Layer: main layers, or one copper layer with its share of the current in %, busiest first (the drawing buttons use the busiest layer for main layers).
  • A buck or boost stage's control loop is one of its responses (the loop gain T): its crossover, phase margin and gain margin are in the stage's tiles and the report, an issue below 45° (30°: an error) or above f_sw/5 (a boost: above a fifth of its right-half-plane zero, D'²·R_load/L, marked RHP). The parts come from the board (the controller by its pin on the switch node; the R-C on its ITH / COMP pin, the divider on its FB pin, the sense resistor between its SENSE pins), the controller's figures from its symbol's fields: Control Mode (peak current, voltage, COT, internal), Vref, EA gm (or EA Gain / EA GBW), Vsense Max and ITH Range (or Current Sense Gain), Slope Compensation, Ramp (voltage mode): see Part parameters from the schematic. Once a controller has a loop field, a field still missing is named in the stage's notes.
  • Bode / poles and zeros views (one per signal path, power stage and PDN rail, after the maps): the frequency response's magnitude and phase on top (× poles and ○ zeros marked at their frequencies), the poles and zeros in the s-plane below (in Hz, on symmetric log axes; the dominant ones labelled with their frequency and Q). A signal path: the voltage at the receivers per volt of the driver, through its lines, parts, filters and op-amp stages (each op-amp stage's own gain too). A power stage: the hot loop's ringing (node current per A of the load pulse: exact poles from L, C_oss, the damping and a snubber), the input transfer (the current reaching the LISN per A of loop current: the input filter's effect), and when the fixes propose an input filter, its attenuation; its output impedance and the converter's input impedance −V²/P (Middlebrook: the first must stay well below the second) are in a second view, "stage impedances" (impedances in Ω, not dB). A PDN rail: each IC's supply impedance (poles at the anti-resonances, zeros at the decaps' series resonances). A response known only from its solves is fitted by a rational function (vector fitting); its largest error is in the tiles and the report, and a coarse fit says so. RHP marks a zero in the right half plane (a line's delay, approximated).
  • Under the tiles, a line sums the view up (also the tiles' tooltip); next to the toolbar the readout shows the value under the mouse. The toolbar is matplotlib's: zoom, pan, save the picture; its Home also undoes a wheel zoom.
  • Log scale is greyed for a temperature view (always linear). Cross-section on a bridge's drive view shows its capacitors' ripple instead.
  • Reading a map: the colour is the current density (by default over three decades, log scale); the arrows are all the same size and point the way the current flows: how many there are shows how much flows (they crowd where it concentrates, idle copper has none). For an AC current (a return, a hot loop) they show the direction when the current is strongest.
  • Maps: the current on each copper layer (a net's return current, a hot loop, a DC path's or a bridge's RMS current density), or the temperature. Arrows show which way the current flows, and their density how much. Green markers show the vias and parts that carry the current between layers, with their share. Log scale shows weak current spread over a plane; untick it to see where most of it flows.
  • Vias on the maps: a green ▲ marks a via that carries the view's current to another layer or net, a ■ a part that does. Every via with at least 1 % of the current is marked (on a DC path or a bridge's RMS map: of the path's current; on a signal route: of its return), drawn at its true size (pad and drill) for the wheel zoom; from 5 % up its share is written next to it. Vias carrying less are left out so a stitched pour does not disappear under markers. In Preferences, Via markers unticked hides them all (parts and issues stay) and Vias shown limits them to the 5, 10, 20 or 50 busiest; it is your preference for every board, and the exported report follows it. Only the drawing changes: the checks (current density, via overload) always look at every via, and the tile busiest via names the one carrying most. On the maps that via has a white ring and its share a white-edged label; shares of vias close together are written apart (with a thin line to their via where they had to move). Click the busiest via tile to zoom the maps to it; opened from KiCad, it is selected there too.
  • Hover over a map for the value under the mouse; over a red / orange issue circle for the issue (severity, check and message, in full as a tooltip). Issues whose circles would overlap at the shown zoom share one circle, in the worst one's colour, with their number beside it: its tooltip counts them and names the worst, a click shows the worst in KiCad, and zooming in parts them; over a via's ▲ or a part's ■ for its name, its share of the current and, on a DC path, a DC load or a bridge's RMS map, its current in amperes. The green ring around a ▲ is the via's own pad, not an issue. Each view marks its own issues: a power stage's DC path view (input, switch node, output, ground) only that path's current-density and via issues, the hot-loop view the stage's other issues.
  • Click an issue circle, a ▲ or a ■ to show it in KiCad, as a click on the Issues tab does: the via, pad or track right at the marker is selected, its net highlighted (never a ground net) and the view zoomed to it, on the map's layer (the zoom and highlight options of the Issues tab apply). An overloaded via's circle shows that via; a ■ shows the part; an issue with no particular copper at its circle (a hot loop, a route's margin) shows the issue's copper as the Issues tab would. The status line says what was shown. Needs GroundTruth started from KiCad.

Remembered for every board; the exported report follows them.
  • Tiles: the view's key numbers, coloured by your rules. Hover over a tile for what it means.
  • Cross-section (signal nets): the return current layer by layer along the route, driver on the left.
  • Spectrum: the emission against the limit, harmonic by harmonic (or a simulated receiver's reading for a bridge).
  • Ctrl + mouse wheel over a map zooms it; double-click shows the whole map; the wheel alone scrolls the page down to the cross-section and the spectrum. Open in window shows the maps in a large window that you can enlarge or maximise; it follows the route, layer and arrows chosen on the tab, and its toolbar zooms and pans all layers together. Open 3D view shows the layers stacked in your browser (the current maps, the signal, the vias and parts carrying the current between layers, and the displacement current between the planes where the model has plane capacitance; drag to turn it: the edge you grab follows the mouse). On a temperature map the contours are isotherms, and there are no arrows; on a bridge's drive view, pick one of its legs first.     • Current density: lines at round values in 1-2-5 steps (2k, 1k, 500, 200, 100 …), over the same three decades below the peak as the heatmap's log colour scale. Every power of ten (1, 10, 100, 1k) is drawn thicker. The values are in the colour bar's unit (A/m per A of signal, or A/mm² for a DC map).
    • Temperature: isotherms in round °C steps from the ambient up (every 5 °C, say), every fifth one thicker.
    • Every layer of a result gets the same levels, so a layer carrying little current gets few lines, as on the heatmap.
    • A line above the copper says what the values are, the step and the peak.
    • Lines stop at the copper's edge and are smoothed within a quarter of a grid cell. Their detail is the simulation grid's: for finer lines press Sharper map, then draw them again (for isotherms, Thermal Quality fine in Preferences).
  • Current capacity: see Thermal and Current capacity.

Fixes tab

Suggest fixes proposes changes for the issues found, each placed where it would be legal on the board, predicted on the solver's model and verified together by simulating again:

  • signal nets: a stitching via at a layer change, a capacitor between two nets at a layer change or across a split, a fill that closes a slot; advice where no part helps;
  • converters and bridge legs: stitching vias at the hot loop's pads, a small capacitor right at the switches; a bridge's own issues (conducted emission, RMS current density) are taken to its legs' candidates.

A fix that would raise the emission somewhere else by more than 1 dB is not selected. Ticked suggestions can be drawn on the board (Draw ticked on board), added to the settings' what-if edits (Add ticked to what-if; then with what-if edits runs with them), or, for vias, placed in KiCad as real vias in one undo step (Place ticked vias in KiCad). Capacitors and fills are never placed for you. Compare what-if simulates the board with and without the enabled what-if edits and lists what changed.

  • The list's columns: #, Kind (via green, cap blue, fill purple), Net, Location, Predicted, Fix, For issue (the check it answers) and Note. Ticked = selected by the search (the best per issue, verified together); tick or untick rows before the buttons. Predicted is the change of the loop moment at the worst harmonic (plane capacitance included) and of the loop inductance at the fundamental.
  • The box under the list shows the verification of the selected fixes (a full analysis on a copy of the board with them), or the text of Compare what-if.
  • Suggest fixes starts from the last Simulate when neither the board, the settings nor the resolution changed since, and its models were kept: they are kept while they take less than a quarter of the memory this computer has free (a large board with every net and stage: ~2 GB). Otherwise it simulates first. That simulation also updates the Issues and Results tabs.
  • Draw ticked on board draws crosses and labels on the suggestion layer. Add ticked to what-if stores the ticked vias, capacitors and fills in the settings and ticks with what-if edits.
  • Place ticked vias in KiCad lists the vias (pad and drill) and asks first; afterwards refill the zones and run DRC. Their what-if edits are switched off ("placed in KiCad"), so a what-if run does not count them twice, and the board is read again.
  • Compare what-if needs enabled what-if edits in the settings (from Add ticked to what-if, or under modifications:).

Design changes (power stages), the second list, fills after every Simulate for each power stage with ringing, radiated or conducted issues. Layout alone rarely clears those; these are the changes that do, each with its parts, the predicted radiated and conducted margin, the overshoot, the extra loss and the issues it clears, best first. The first row is the stage as it is. They are worked out on the stage's own solves (the loop, its moment and its input path stay), in moments:

  • RC snubber across the switch node (C a few times C_oss, R about √(L/C_oss)): it damps the ringing; its resistor burns C·V²·f_sw, so the list gives the best-damping one and the lowest-loss one that meets the overshoot target.
  • Slower edge (a turn-on gate resistor): less spectrum above 1/(π·t_r) and less overshoot, for ½·V·I·Δt·f_sw more switching loss.
  • Shorter hot loop: the largest loop inductance that meets the targets, if a layout can reach it (about 0.4× today's at best, not below 0.4 nH); Suggest fixes searches the capacitor and via positions.
  • Together: when no single change clears both the ringing and the radiated issue, the cheapest mix of a snubber, a slower edge and a shorter loop that does.
  • Input π-filter at the connector: L and C from standard values (the capacitor's DC-bias loss assumed), its damping on whichever side keeps it stable against the converter's input impedance (Middlebrook), its attenuation from the stage's input capacitors and the LISN.
  • Spread spectrum: the best of a few modulations through the simulated EMI receiver, where the controller has it.
  • Common mode: the switch node's stray-capacitance budget, and the choke and Y capacitors needed for 1, 10 and 50 pF to a chassis.

The targets: margins of at least rules.emi_margin_db, and an overshoot within rules.overshoot_warning of V_in, or within 80 % of the switches' rating where they carry a Vds field (Part parameters from the schematic). Select a row for its parts and notes; Check in Simulate simulates that stage again with a snubber, edge time or spread spectrum applied (in memory: the settings stay as they are) and shows the prediction next to the result. The exported report has the same list under each stage (How to fix it).

Thermal and Current capacity

Thermal (top bar, after Simulate) solves the copper and part temperatures with the heat of every converter, every motor bridge and every DC load from the last Simulate (a DC load's current capacity scales all its loads together). It reads the board first: if the board or the settings changed since that Simulate, it says so and asks for a Simulate instead of heating the design as it was:

  • copper: a converter's DC paths, a bridge's RMS currents (Joule heat, resistivity following the temperature);
  • parts: their losses at the operating point (switch conduction, switching edges, output charge, a synchronous rectifier's reverse conduction in the dead times and its recovery charge, an inductor's DCR, series parts; for a bridge, the losses from its drive waveform).

Current capacity (Results tab) finds, per converter, the load current, and per bridge, the phase current, at which the hottest copper or part junction reaches the limit (thermal.capacity_c, by default max_c), the others at their operating points. The thermal settings: ambient temperature, orientation, natural or forced convection (air speed), the warning and maximum temperatures, and the parts' junction-to-board resistances (theta_jb). The thermal mesh is the settings' thermal.quality (draft, normal, fine) unless Preferences sets one for this computer. Both run in the background; Cancel stops them. The solve is steady state: a load that lasts a few seconds (a cranking pulse) heats less than shown.

Thermal adds a temperature view to the Results tab's Route list (replacing an earlier one), its issues join the Issues tab, and the status line gives the hottest copper and its layer and the power in copper and parts. The map shows each of its cells at the hottest point the solve found in it, so a track narrower than a map cell shows its own temperature. The copper's heat is exactly the DC paths' I²·R, its resistivity following the temperature, in the copper itself (also where a grid cell at the copper's edge has its point just outside it); a converter adds its inductor's ripple, the switching current its input capacitors send around the hot loop, and the heat in every via barrel the current passes. One convection coefficient per face, from the face's mean temperature (near a hot spot the real one is a little higher, so the estimate is on the warm side). The DC tables stay at 20 °C; Thermal's notes give each DC path at its own temperature (its copper's resistance, drop and loss). A long, narrow, hot track can use up the normal quality's mesh budget part-way along it (a note says so): the rest of it reads a few per cent cool there, and a track narrower than the finest cell (0.05 mm on normal) a few per cent cool too; Thermal Quality fine resolves both.

Current capacity adds one view per stage or bridge at its capacity (replacing earlier capacity views), and the status line lists each one's capacity and what limits it.

Labelling a signal path

Detect finds most clock and data paths on its own, from net names and pin types. Label a path when it:

  • looks like a supply (a name with RAIL, VIN or +3V3 in it, or a power pin on the net), for example an ADC input that measures a rail;
  • gets the wrong driver (both ends are bidirectional pins, or the schematic has no pin types);
  • runs through an op-amp. Detect only crosses an op-amp that is labelled, or set to amplifier on the Parts tab;
  • runs through a resistor above 1 kΩ or a capacitor, which a path doesn't cross unless it is labelled or named in Pass through.

What to label. Label only the parts at the ends of the path and its op-amps:

PartGT fieldMeaning
the part that drives the pathsig_drv:3pin 3 drives the signal
each op-amp on the wayamp_in:1, amp_out:4the stage's input pin (non-inverting input for a follower) and its output pin; the part becomes an amplifier in the Parts tab
the part that receives itsig_rx:9pin 9 receives it

Leave the resistors, ferrites and filter capacitors in between unlabelled: the path finds them itself. sig:9 (no direction) only says the net on pin 9 is a signal; Detect then guesses its driver. sig on its own, on a 2-pin part, marks both its nets as signals.

Pins are pad numbers (sig_rx:9) or pin names (sig_rx:PA2), as in the schematic symbol. For a dual or quad op-amp, name the pins of the stage on the path (amp_in:5, amp_out:7 for the second unit). Several labels on one part go in the same field, separated by commas: a microcontroller with two ADC inputs on two paths gets sig_rx:9, sig_rx:10.

How Detect turns the labels into a path. It starts at a labelled net and follows 2-pin parts and labelled op-amps (input net to output net) to the next labelled net. It never crosses into a ground, a reference net or an unlabelled supply. Branches that end at an unlabelled net (a load resistor to ground, a filter capacitor) are dropped from the path. They still count in Simulate, as parts across the path. Parts on the path that don't pass a signal by default (a resistor over 1 kΩ) go into its Pass through list. The path is named after one of its nets with a real name (not Net-(…)). One row then covers every net of the path. Op-amps get their gain from a Gain field on the symbol or from the Parts tab (1 when neither is given). Don't put a sig label on an op-amp in the path: it would end the path there. Use amp_in / amp_out instead.

Example (a current-sense output read by an ADC through a buffer and a resistor):

U302 (current-sense amplifier)sig_drv:3
U301 (op-amp follower)amp_in:1, amp_out:4
R303 4.7 kΩ, C304 100 nF to groundno label
U1102 (microcontroller)sig_rx:9

Detect gives one path, U302.3 → U301 ×1 → R303 4k7 → U1102.9, named after the ADC net, with R303 in Pass through and C304 as a part across the path.

Steps:

  1. In the schematic editor, add a field named GT to each symbol, either in Symbol Properties or, for several at once, in the Symbol Fields Table (add a GT column).
  2. Update PCB from Schematic (F8) and save the board. The labels reach the board as footprint fields and change no net names.
  3. In GroundTruth, press Reload board, then Detect…. The new path is ticked. An unreviewed row that covered part of it before is offered as REPLACES: keep it ticked and press OK. Reviewed and hand-made rows are never changed; edit or remove them yourself.
  4. Set the path's frequency and rise time on the Signal paths tab, then mark it reviewed.

Shortcut: select one bit of copper on each net of the path in the PCB editor and press Add KiCad selection (see A whole path from KiCad). GroundTruth works out the same labels and adds the path at once.

Note: writing the GT labels into the schematic automatically needs KiCad 11. KiCad 10's API can't change a schematic, so on KiCad 10 GroundTruth shows the labels in a message for you to add by hand (step 1; Ctrl+C copies the message). The path is in the settings either way, and Simulate uses it right away; the labels only keep it in the design, so that Detect finds it again later.

The line at the bottom of the Converters tab (Labels (GT field)) names the parts that carry signal and op-amp labels, and lists as problems a GT word that is not a label (a typing error), a label naming a pin its part doesn't have, and an op-amp with amp_in but no amp_out (or the other way round). If a labelled path still doesn't appear in Detect…, check that every net of the path is joined to the next by a 2-pin part or a labelled op-amp.

The settings file

GroundTruth keeps everything for a board in one folder next to it, groundtruth, so the KiCad project folder stays tidy: the settings file, the simulation history and (offered first) the exported reports. Everything you enter is kept in groundtruth\<board>.groundtruth.yaml: the nets, converters, bridges, part models, rules (thresholds, severities), analysis settings, waivers and what-if edits. Commit it with the board. It can be edited by hand; the plugin reads it when the window opens. The same file drives the groundtruth command line for CI, with the same results.

Useful analysis settings: max_nodes (the grid budget of each model: lower it for speed or memory), threads (solves in parallel), conducted (cispr32, cispr25, mil461 or none) and conducted_class, limit_class (CISPR 32 class A or B for radiated), radiated_limit (another radiated limit: MIL-STD-461G RE102 by name, e.g. re102_ground_army or re102_aircraft_external (fixed wing, from 2 MHz) or re102_helicopter (from 10 kHz), at 1 m with the standard's bandwidths; or a curve of your own: a name, the distance and its points), mil461 (the MIL-STD-461 revision, G or H: under H the ships' CE101 limit runs to 20 kHz; nothing else here differs), ce101 (MIL-STD-461 CE101, the input leads' current from 30 Hz to 10 kHz, ships under 461H to 20 kHz: aircraft or ships; a motor bridge's commutation lines, strongest at six times the electrical frequency, read through the bus capacitors and the test leads, which can resonate in this band; shown as CE101 margin and in the emission plots), gpu (auto, on, off). In the plugin, Preferences sets the resolution and can replace threads, gpu and thermal.quality on this computer; those choices are not written to the file.

Speed, memory and the GPU

  • A board with a few nets simulates in a minute or two; a power stage takes 30–70 s; a motor bridge with its victims and a sweep several minutes.
  • Large models need memory: each bridge model on an 8-layer board can take a few GB while it is factorised. When the models of all nets and stages would not fit in the free memory at once, Simulate solves them in groups, one after the other (the board-wide model last); the results are the same, it may take a little longer. Simulate does not stop when memory runs short: before each large factorisation it checks what it will need against what is free (on a compute server, within the job's limit) and frees the factorisations it has kept, waits for the others being made, or makes a leaner one (the least fill: more iterations, the same results). A solve that runs out anyway is repeated on its own with everything freed, then with the leaner factorisation; only if even that fails is that one frequency left unsolved (a note, and a band_unsolved warning naming what it misses), and the rest of the run finishes. The run's notes say what it had to do. To avoid it: close other programs, choose fewer Solver threads or a lower Resolution (above 1×) in Preferences, or lower analysis.max_nodes.
  • With PyTorch installed in the plugin's environment on a machine with an NVIDIA GPU (CUDA) or an AMD Radeon (ROCm, e.g. a Radeon 860M), the solver's iterations run on the GPU (GPU in Preferences, or analysis.gpu: auto). The status line ends with "solved on GPU <name>". The installer is tools/install_gpu.py in the plugin folder (--amd for a Radeon), run once with KiCad's Python while KiCad is closed; tools/check_gpu.py checks it and says whether the GPU is faster than the CPU. An integrated GPU shares the computer's memory and computes in double precision far slower than in single precision, and so does a GeForce card (1/64 of its single-precision speed), so it may not be. GPU auto therefore times the solver's own work on the GPU against the CPU once per session and uses the GPU only when it is at least 1.5 times as fast; otherwise the status line says "solved on CPU (the <GPU> computes … at 0.3x the CPU's speed here …)". On uses the GPU anyway. Only one program at a time computes on a GPU: under auto, a second one (another Simulate, a compute server's other job) runs on the CPU and says the GPU is in use; under on it waits for it.
  • The window never waits for a job: everything slow runs in the background, and Cancel stops it.

How the simulation works (the algorithms)

Simulate uses one field solver for everything electrical. Nets, converters, DC paths, bridges and the fix search differ only in the current they drive into it. Thermal has a solver of its own. Where each formula and method comes from, and what the model assumes, is in References and assumptions.

This is not FEM. The field solver uses PEEC (partial element equivalent circuit), an integral-equation method from the method-of-moments family. Only the copper is meshed: each layer's copper is cut into small strips, and vias, pins and parts become branches between layers. Each strip gets its resistance, its partial inductance to every other strip (from the free-space field), and the capacitance between facing planes. The board becomes one large circuit, solved at each frequency. The thermal and DC solves come from the PCB Thermal solver: a finite-volume grid, not FEM either.

PEEC and FEM compared. FEM (the finite element method) meshes the whole volume: copper, dielectric and the air around the board, out to an artificial boundary. Its system is sparse but very large. PEEC meshes only the conductors and puts the coupling through space into its kernels, so there is no air mesh. Its system is smaller but dense, which a regular grid lets it apply with FFTs.

  • Why PEEC here. A PCB is thin copper (35 µm) spread over a large area. FEM would need millions of flat elements and a robust mesher for any KiCad geometry. PEEC gives the copper's currents directly, and those are the questions GroundTruth answers: return paths, loop inductance, hot loops, plane resonances and DC paths. It is also fast enough to solve dozens of frequencies per net and to try every candidate fix. Full-wave FEM is slow at the low frequencies where power stages live (DC to a few MHz). Commercial power- and signal-integrity tools use PEEC, method-of-moments or 2.5D solvers for the same reasons.
  • Where FEM (or another full 3D method such as FDTD) would shine. Radiation with the enclosure and the cables in the model: GroundTruth estimates radiated emission from the board's current loops alone. Local 3D detail: current crowding inside a big connector pin or a dense via field, or skin and proximity effects in thick copper. Independent reference solutions, to check where PEEC's quasi-static, planar assumptions stop holding at high frequency (see What the numbers mean, and their limits).

The field solver: quasi-static PEEC (partial element equivalent circuit, in the frequency domain)

  • The grid. Every plane layer is cut into the same square grid. Copper is sampled on a finer sub-grid (≤ 0.15 mm), so thin webs between via antipads stay connected and a slot still cuts. Tracks narrower than a cell are followed along their centre line and kept joined; where another net's pour takes a cell, the track crosses it as a lumped branch with the track's own resistance and inductance. Nets are never joined, even when their clearance is smaller than a cell, and neither are two pieces of one net's copper that do not touch on the layer. Each strip conducts with exactly the width of copper that crosses its cell border (a track at an angle crosses it w / cos θ long, which keeps its resistance), and each node's current density is taken over its cell's own copper, so that the heat it gives Thermal is exactly the strips' I²·R.
  • The unknowns:
    • the currents in the x and y strips between cells on every layer;
    • the currents through the plane-pair capacitances, the vias, the plated pads and the parts;
    • the voltage of every node.
    The equations are Kirchhoff's voltage law over each strip and branch, Z·I − A·V = −jω·ψ (ψ: the field of the known signal or switch current), and Kirchhoff's current law at each node.
  • The partial elements:
    • a strip has R(f), including skin effect (the exact result for a copper slab with the field on one face, as over its return plane) and the copper thickness from the stackup, plus jωL;
    • every strip couples magnetically to every other strip on every layer (mutual partial inductance);
    • two layers with copper and none in between form a parallel-plate capacitance ε₀·εᵣ·area/t, scaled by the cell's copper fraction and with dielectric loss (tan δ), the dielectrics between them in series;
    • a via is its barrel resistance plus partial self-inductance; a through-hole pin (a connector's) is a solid conductor joined to every layer all around its barrel, so its current enters the copper around the whole ring, not at one point;
    • a part is its model's Z(f) plus 0.3 nH mounting inductance, spread over its pads.
    The integrals use Gauss–Legendre quadrature near by, filaments far away, and numerical integration for the self term.
  • Fast products. The mutual inductance between two strips depends only on their offset, so each layer pair is a convolution. It is applied with FFTs (double precision; single precision stalled the solver near resonances) and a compiled multiply-add.
  • The linear solve: GMRES (right-preconditioned, restarted):
    • Gram–Schmidt orthogonalisation, repeated only where cancellation happens;
    • Givens rotations keep the small least-squares problem triangular;
    • the restart length follows a memory budget;
    • a solve that stops converging is marked unsolved, and its band is named in the results, never silently filled in.
  • The preconditioner is an exact sparse factorisation (SuperLU) of the system's Schur complement Aᵀ·D⁻¹·A. D has two forms:
    • each strip's own impedance, for the route models, which are inductive;
    • per grid position, a block that couples all layers, for the board-wide model. It keeps the planes' cavity modes; without it the solver needs thousands of iterations above about 600 MHz.
  • Reuse.
    • One factorisation serves every net at its frequency. Nearby frequencies (within ±25 %) borrow it within an iteration budget; past that budget the solver factorises again.
    • The plan of which frequency borrows what is fixed before solving, so the same board gives bit-identical results whatever the thread count.
    • Candidate vias and capacitors (Suggest fixes) and a converter's LISN are added as low-rank (Woodbury) updates of the existing factorisation, and their solves start from the baseline solution.
    • With a GPU, the FFTs, products and orthogonalisation run there; the factorisations stay on the CPU (see Speed, memory and the GPU).

Signal nets

  • Two models per net, because one grid can't be both fine enough for the loop next to the trace and large enough for the whole board:
    • a fine model around the route, with R + jωL;
    • a coarser model of the whole board that adds the plane capacitance, for displacement currents and plane resonances.
    Their loop moments combine as m(f) = mroute(f0) + [mboard(f) − mboard(f0)], so the coarse grid's bias at f0 cancels.
  • The current on the trace. The driver is a Thévenin source (resistance, inductance, capacitance) with the trapezoid's harmonics. The route is a chain of lossless transmission lines (Z0 and εeff of each stretch from the board's own cross-section there, solved as the Signal integrity checks solve it: the copper around the track, the stackup's dielectrics and the solder mask; closed forms where no clean cut exists) and series parts, solved at each harmonic and loaded by the receivers. A net's current_a replaces this: the line's peak current at the fundamental. Shunt parts and op-amp stages become current loops of their own, added per harmonic with their own currents.
  • Frequencies. The solves happen at actual harmonics of the signal. The solver then adds solves in the gaps where a resonance could hide or where the estimate is closest to the limit, and always solves the worst-margin harmonic itself. Between solves, |m| is interpolated on log-log axes.
  • Emission. A small loop radiates |E| = 1.316·10⁻¹⁴·f²·|m|·I/r, doubled for the ground reflection. The receiver's bandwidth adds harmonics that fall in it in phase. The result is compared with CISPR 32.

Power stages and bridges

  • The hot loop. The switches are current filaments carrying the commutated current as a trapezoid pulse train; their pours are solved copper.
    • The loop inductance comes from a series-LC fit at 30 and 100 MHz.
    • The ringing is a second-order resonance with C_oss, and an edge-charge factor adds the C_oss·dv/dt current. It was checked against a time-domain simulation of the switching cell.
  • Conducted emission. The LISN is added to the loop as a low-rank update. Its current, per ampere of loop current, is interpolated over frequency and summed over the receiver bandwidth. For spread spectrum, the receiver itself is simulated: each harmonic's sweep passes a Gaussian filter swept across the band, then the standard's peak or average detector. Common mode is C·dV/dt of the switch node through the two LISNs.
  • DC paths are the same solver at 10 Hz, where the copper is purely resistive. All stages share one model of the whole board, so their currents add (superposition) in the "currents together" view. Via currents are checked against IPC-2221.
  • Fix search. Candidates are ranked one by one. They are then picked greedily: in each round the remaining candidates are re-evaluated with the chosen ones in place, and the best is added.

Thermal (the PCB Thermal solver)

  • Steady-state 3-D heat conduction on a block-structured mesh, refined where the heat is. Convection and radiation leave through the board's faces (correlations for h).
  • The heat comes from two sources:
    • the copper's Joule heat ρ·|J|²·t from the DC current maps;
    • the parts' losses: conduction, the switching edges, Q_oss·V·f, a diode's V_f, the inductor's DCR.
  • The system is solved with conjugate gradients, preconditioned by algebraic multigrid (pyamg; a direct solve for small models). The copper's resistivity follows each node's temperature, by iteration.

Each model is checked against an analytic result in the test suite. Examples: a microstrip's return-current spread and loop inductance against Wheeler, a pour over its plane against Hammerstad, parallel-plate capacitance and the cavity's first resonance, current conservation across every cut between planes, and a strip's I²R heating.

What the numbers mean, and their limits

  • Emission figures are for ranking and comparing layouts, not a compliance prediction. The model is quasi-static (no radiation from plane edges), it estimates the differential-mode loop of each net or hot loop, and common-mode currents on cables, which often dominate real emissions, are not modelled (a converter's common-mode budget is the exception).
  • The board-wide model is quasi-static: above the frequency at which the board is a quarter wavelength across in its laminate (about 350 MHz for 100 mm of FR-4) its emissions are indicative upper bounds. The spectrum shades that range grey, the issue says so, and a finer Resolution than 4× buys no accuracy there. analysis.max_frequency in the settings file (e.g. 1GHz; auto = the edge's own band, a decade above 1/(π·rise time)) cuts the band evaluated: faster runs, or a test plan's own top; the range above it is hatched and the notes say it was not computed, not that it passed.
  • Plane resonance peaks are upper estimates (the model has no radiation loss); margins set by a resonance are good to a few dB.
  • The edge time sets the high-frequency content and L·di/dt: use the fastest edge at the operating current (a GaN bridge at 100 A switches in about a nanosecond, at 1 A on a bench in 100 ns).
  • Thermal: steady state; heat leaves through the board's faces only (no heatsinks, enclosure or part bodies yet), so large parts come out on the hot side.
  • Results depend on what you enter: frequencies, edge times, currents, part models. Orange rows are still guesses.
  • Each assumption the model makes is listed, with its reason, under References and assumptions.
