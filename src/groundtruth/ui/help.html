GroundTruth help

GroundTruth checks a KiCad board for return-path problems on its signals, and analyses its switching power stages and motor bridges: hot loops, emission, currents in the copper, losses and temperatures. This page explains what each part of the window does and how the analyses fit together. F1 (or Help in the top bar) opens it at the tab you are on; pressed again, the same help window comes forward at the new tab's section. Links within this page jump to their section; others open in your web browser. The sidebar lists every section and marks the one you are reading; Search (Ctrl+F) shows the sections that mention a word, the word marked (Enter: the next one).

The usual workflow

  1. Open the board. The first time, GroundTruth looks for the nets worth analysing, the DC-DC converters and the motor bridges by itself. What it found shows in orange on the Signal paths, Converters and Bridges tabs until you review it.
  2. Review. Check each row: the driver and receivers of a net, a converter's switches, inductor and operating point, a bridge's legs. Fill in what the board cannot tell: frequencies, edge times, currents, voltages, a motor's data. Press Mark reviewed.
  3. Run checks (seconds): rule checks on the nets' routes against the planes.
  4. Simulate (minutes): the field solver on everything listed. Results go to the Issues and Results tabs. Both first check the settings against the board: a net, pin or part the board no longer has stops the run with what to fix. A reference net (a ground such as GND2) deleted or renamed in KiCad is offered for removal instead: Yes takes it out of the settings (with none left, the grounds found on the board), one Undo step.
  5. Look and fix: click an issue to see it in KiCad, look at the current maps, let Suggest fixes (Fixes tab) propose vias and capacitors, try them as what-if edits, place the vias.
  6. Thermal and Current capacity for power stages and bridges, after Simulate.

Every edit on the tabs is saved to the settings file at once, and Undo (Ctrl+Z) takes it back. What is set up can be kept as a configuration and loaded again later to run the same simulation (see Configurations and Undo). The board itself is never changed, except when you place suggested vias or draw on the board.

The top bar, left to right: Reload board reads the board again (after you changed it in KiCad); Refill zones has KiCad refill every zone, waits until it has finished (on a complex board minutes: the status line counts them; KiCad answers nothing meanwhile) and then reloads; Run checks, Simulate and Export report… (below); with what-if edits; Cancel stops the running job; Configuration ▾ (save and load configurations, Back to last settings), Undo and Redo (Configurations and Undo); Clear GT markers removes what GroundTruth drew. On the right, Preferences… and Help. Refill zones and Clear GT markers are there only when GroundTruth was started from KiCad's toolbar (Analyse return paths); opened on a board file, it can analyse but not show or draw anything in KiCad. While a job runs the buttons are greyed. While the board is read (at start, after Reload or Refill, and while new boards' nets are detected) every button but Preferences, Help and Cancel is greyed, and hovering over one says "Please wait for the board to be loaded". A button whose action needs an earlier run is greyed until there is one, and hovering says which: the Results tab's actions (Draw contours, Draw arrows, Draw hot loop, Open 3D view, Sharper map, Thermal, Current capacity) "Please run the simulation first", Export report… and the Issues tab's Draw markers the checks or the simulation, the Fixes tab's Draw / Place / Add ticked Suggest fixes. Hover over Export report…, with what-if edits, Preferences… or Help for what it does.

The results shown are always of the board and settings they were computed on. When the board was read again and differs (Reload board, or a Simulate that found it changed in KiCad), or a setting the results depend on was changed, the Results tab's summary line starts with OUT OF DATE and says which; Simulate again for the current design. The thermal settings (ambient, convection, limits) are not among them: Thermal reads them when it runs.

Starting GroundTruth. In KiCad's PCB editor the plugin has three actions:

  • Analyse return paths (toolbar button) opens the window on the board open in the PCB editor. A small start window shows at once ("Connecting to KiCad…", "Loading the analysis modules…") and closes when the main window opens. If KiCad cannot be reached or no board is open, a message says so.
  • Add selected net to GroundTruth (toolbar button) adds the net of the track, via or pad selected in the PCB editor to the Signal paths tab (see Signal paths). An open GroundTruth window of the same board takes it and comes to the front (while it runs a Simulate or another job, once that has finished); with none open, a window opens and adds it once it has read the board. With nothing selected that has a net, a message says what KiCad reports as selected.
  • Clear GT markers (no toolbar button) removes what GroundTruth drew on the board, without opening the window, and says how many items it removed (one undo step).

Without KiCad, groundtruth-gui BOARD.kicad_pcb opens the same window on a board file: everything analyses the same, but the KiCad parts are left out (Refill zones, Clear GT markers, Add KiCad selection, the zoom and highlight options, the drawing buttons, showing rows and issues in KiCad, the Parts tab following KiCad's selection). Started from KiCad, the window closes by itself when KiCad exits.

When the board is read, the board must have been saved once (its settings go into the groundtruth folder next to it). A settings file with errors is reported and nothing is loaded. When some zones are not filled, GroundTruth asks whether to refill them first. When the settings file is new or lists no nets, converters or bridges, detection runs by itself (a settings file from before the Bridges tab is searched for bridges once). Closing the window saves the settings and clears the net highlight it set in KiCad, giving back your active layer.

The status bar shows what happened last; a long message wraps onto up to three lines (hover over it for all of it). While a job runs it shows what the job is doing, how far it is, the time spent and about how long is left, with a progress bar on the right; a job of 10 s or more says afterwards how long it took. During Simulate's solves the percent moves by the solves counted, "solve k of ≤ n": n is the most solves the run can still take (each model's planned frequencies and the most its refinement may add); when a model's refinement ends early, n drops and the bar jumps forward. Model building before the solves and finishing after them take their own part of the bar, so the percent is a few points off k / n. Only one job runs at a time: a button pressed meanwhile is refused with "still working". Cancel stops Run checks, Simulate, Thermal, Current capacity, Suggest fixes, Compare what-if and Export report at their next progress step; reading the board, detection, drawing and Sharper map cannot be cancelled. A settings file that cannot be written is reported there too ("could not save the settings").

What Simulate does with nets, converters and bridges

Everything listed is simulated, every time. Simulate runs every net on the Signal paths tab, every converter on the Converters tab and every motor bridge on the Bridges tab, in one job. To leave something out, remove its row (the detection can find it again later) or keep a second settings file. The command line (groundtruth simulate --nets NAME) can run some of them.

They share the board, not the physics. All of them use one model of the board's copper, read once, and their solves run in parallel. Each one is still solved as if it were alone:

A netA signal from its driver to its receivers at its frequency and edge time. The solver finds where its return current flows in the planes (under the trace, around a slot, through a stitching via or a capacitor, between planes through their capacitance), the loop it makes, and the emission that loop radiates at each harmonic.
A converterIts hot loop: the fast current that moves between the switches and the input capacitors at every edge, at its switching frequency. Also its DC power paths (current density and resistance), its ringing, the conducted emission at its input connector and the radiated estimate of the loop.
A motor bridgeFirst its six-step drive with the motor, at the operating point: the phase, switch and bus currents. Then each leg's hot loop at the leg's largest switched current, all legs on one model. Together: the conducted emission of all legs at the battery input, the bus capacitors' ripple, the RMS current density from the battery to the motor, the parts' losses, and the sweep over other currents and speeds.

No switching current is fed into the nets' solves. A net on the Signal paths tab shows its own return path, not the noise a converter or a bridge puts on it. That interaction has its own analysis:

  • Victims of a bridge or a converter. When a bridge or a power stage is simulated, GroundTruth finds the microcontroller, reset, crystal, Hall, debug and PWM / gate-input nets on the board by itself (or takes the ones listed under victims: in the settings, picked with Victims… on the Bridges tab), puts them into the stage's model and computes the noise each one's receiver sees at the switching edges. An input into a Schmitt trigger (a logic buffer with a Hysteresis, or Threshold Rising and Falling) is a victim by itself too, and its noise is checked against that hysteresis instead of the flat limit [A75]. This does not depend on the Signal paths tab. The results are in the bridge's drive view, in each stage's part of the report, and as victim_coupling issues.

Where the results come together:

  • Issues lists every problem of every net, converter and bridge, sorted by severity.
  • Results has a view per net route, converter, DC path, bridge, bridge leg, RMS map and sweep.
  • Thermal and Current capacity add the heat of every converter and every bridge on the board: they share the copper.
  • Suggest fixes works through all the issues and proposes fixes per net, converter or bridge leg; a fix is checked on every net it could disturb.

Run checks and Simulate

Run checks follows each listed net from its driver to its receivers (through series resistors and ferrites) and checks every segment against the planes next to it: crossing a gap or a split, running off a plane or along its edge, changing layer without a stitching via (same reference net) or a capacitor (different nets), referencing a non-ground plane. It also runs the board-wide voltage-aware safety checks (spacing for the voltage, capacitor ratings, ESD parts at connectors). It takes seconds.

Simulate runs the checks and then the field solver on everything listed (see above). The status line shows what it is doing, how far it is and the time left; Cancel stops it. With with what-if edits ticked, both run on a copy of the board with the enabled what-if edits (virtual vias, capacitors, fills) added; with none enabled, it says where to add them.

Run checks without nets runs the board-wide checks only; Simulate needs a net, a converter, a bridge, a DC load or a PDN rail. Afterwards the status line counts the errors, warnings and infos (waived ones left out) and the window turns to the Issues tab (Run checks) or the Results tab (Simulate).

The settings are checked first. A value that looks like a missing unit is refused with what to write instead (a C_oss of 482 is 482 F: write 482p); a bridge without its operating point or motor data is listed with what is missing.

Signal paths tab

The signal paths to analyse, one row each: a path runs from its driver pin to its receiver pins, across nets through series parts (0R, termination resistors, ferrites), the parts named in Pass through and op-amp stages, and is solved as one. Each row has its frequency, edge time and amplitude (optional: source and load; in the settings file also current_a, the line's peak current at the fundamental, and driver_return / receiver_return, the pins the return is taken at). A path on one net is simply a path without parts.

The table's columns: Path (named after its start net), Status, Chain (the path as the route follows it: U42.3 → U41 ×1 → R41 4k7 → U53.9, op-amps with their gain; worked out in the background, "no route: …" when the copper or a part doesn't connect), Freq, Rise, Ampl. and Class (the kind of signal detection took it for). Under the table, the selected path in full: its chain and its Branches: per net of the path, every two-pin part hanging off it to another net (a filter capacitor or a pull-down to GND, a pull-up to a rail), marked ✗ … (DNP) when it is not fitted. Those to a reference net (not DNP) take their share of the current in Simulate. Status is manual (entered by you), auto, REVIEW (found by detection, not checked yet: the row is orange) or auto, reviewed.

Where Detect misses a path, takes it for a supply, guesses its driver wrong or stops at an op-amp, label the path in the schematic: see Labelling a signal path.

  • The form: Start net (pick it from the list of the board's nets or type it); then Driver pin and Receiver pins list the pins of that net (also across series parts) to choose from; Frequency, Rise time, Amplitude (V) and Duty cycle (0.5 for a clock). Values take units and SI prefixes (20MHz, 2ns, 3.3).
  • A signal path across nets: the driver and receiver pins may be on different nets joined by series parts (a damping resistor, a ferrite, an inductor): the path runs through them and each part's impedance (from the Parts tab) counts, at its place along the path. A part that doesn't pass a signal by default (an AC-coupling capacitor, a resistor above 1 kΩ) is passed when named in Pass through (e.g. C12); when no path is found, the issue names the parts in the way. The source: Source R (Ω), Source L (nH, package and bond wire) and Source C (pF, its output capacitance), Fall time when it differs from the rise time. The load: Load C (pF per receiver) and Load R (Ω, a termination at the receivers). Empty fields use the defaults (25 Ω, 5 pF, no L, C or termination). The report shows the path per net: its stretches and parts with their |Z| at the worst harmonic. Parts from the path to ground or a rail (a termination, an RC or pi filter's capacitor, a pull-down) are found on their own: each takes its share of the current at its place, and one that carries at least 1 % of it is a current loop of its own (the return flows back through the plane from its ground pin), solved with the net. The report lists them with their current; the notes name the loops. An op-amp on the path (part model amplifier, see Parts tab) splits it into stages: the driver drives the op-amp's input, the op-amp drives the rest with its gain (rolled off at its bandwidth or GBW / gain, capped by its slew rate) through its output impedance, and each side's current returns at the op-amp's ground pin: two current loops. The maps then show the output side's.
  • Detect… suggests nets from their names and the schematic's pin types (SPI, RMII, USB, SDIO, debug clocks, crystals …), with the driver from the pin types and the series parts followed, and default timing to review. A dialog lists what it found that is not listed yet (with its class, driver, receivers, timing, its Confidence and Why); the likely ones are ticked. OK adds the ticked nets as unreviewed. A net already listed, still unreviewed and detected, whose path detection now sees differently (after GT labels were added, say) is offered again, ticked and marked REPLACES: it replaces the entry (its tick kept), and unreviewed entries for pieces of its path are removed. Reviewed and hand-made nets are never changed.
  • A path between two pads: select just the two pads at its ends in the PCB editor (Ctrl+click the second), then Add KiCad selection. GroundTruth traces every route between them: through any passive part with two pins (a capacitor or a large resistor too: those go into Pass through; a resistor, capacitor, inductor, ferrite, fuse or diode; not a two-pin connector, whose pins are not joined inside it), and through op-amps, comparators and logic buffers from input to output where their GT labels or pin names say which pins those are (two channels of one package one after the other too); never through a ground or a supply. The direction is the one a signal can take (into an op-amp's input), else from the pad more likely to drive. One route is added at once; for several you pick one from a list, the shortest first (of equal length, the one needing fewer Pass through parts first); on a dense board the list holds the 50 shortest. With no route (an op-amp still without labels on the way), the two nets are added on their own and the status line says why. To see every route and everything hanging on it (bias stages, dividers, decoupling) without adding anything: tools/dev/path_map.py BOARD --from U4.11 --to U1.9.
  • A whole path from KiCad: select one bit of copper on each net of the path in the PCB editor (a track, via or pad per net, Ctrl+click to add, any order), then Add KiCad selection or the toolbar button. The nets are put in order by the parts that join them; an op-amp's input and output pins give the direction (else the pin types at the ends; a selected pad fixes that end's pin). One row is added: driver → series parts and op-amps → receivers, the resistors that pass no signal by default in Pass through, the op-amps modelled as amplifiers with their two pins (Parts tab). The GT labels this means for the schematic symbols (sig_drv, amp_in / amp_out, sig_rx, see labels) are written into the open schematic where KiCad allows it. That needs KiCad 11 (see the note): on KiCad 10 they are shown in a message to add by hand (Symbol Fields Table, a GT column; Ctrl+C copies the message). The path works in Simulate right away either way. Selected nets no part joins are added one by one.
  • Bode sweeps the selected paths (every ticked one when none is selected) with a sine, from Sweep from to Sweep to on Sweep points log-spaced frequencies (empty: a hundredth of the frequency to the top of its harmonics' band, 300 points; Update selected keeps them in the settings as sweep_from, sweep_to, sweep_points). It solves the path's whole circuit as Simulate does (see Op-amp circuits and the transfer function), without the plane models: seconds. The Results tab gets one Bode / poles and zeros view per path (replacing its earlier one): the receiver's voltage per volt of the driver, the output and loop gain of each op-amp channel the signal passes (a quad's other channels, or a bias buffer tied to the path, stay in the circuit for their loading but out of the plot; a note names them, and a loop of theirs that rings is still flagged), as magnitude and phase, the poles and zeros, the transfer function written out, and the tiles: gain (at the sweep's start: the DC gain when it starts low enough, e.g. 1 Hz for a sensor or ADC input; its −3 dB frequency, the bandwidth; a peak above it), phase margin (the op-amps' loops), then the dominant poles. The command line has groundtruth bode (--from, --to, --points, --json, --plots).
  • Add KiCad selection (or KiCad's toolbar button Add selected net to GroundTruth) adds the net of the track, via or pad selected in the PCB editor (click or right-click it there first), as an unreviewed suggestion with its driver and receivers from the pin types; the status line says what was added, and asks for the frequency and rise time where the net's kind of signal is unknown. A net of unknown kind driven by an op-amp with a Slew Rate or GBW / Bandwidth field (an analog output, a buffered sensor) gets the fastest full swing that op-amp can make instead of 10 MHz / 2 ns: a TLV6001 (0.5 V/µs) gives 5.28 µs edges at 48.2 kHz. Set the real signal if it is slower. Ground nets and power-stage switch nodes are refused with the reason. A net that looks like a supply (a power pin on it, a supply name such as +3V3, VIN, RAIL) or carries a power stage's current is refused too, but you are asked whether to add it as a signal anyway: an ADC input or a sense line measuring a rail is one. A name with a measuring word (ADC, SENSE, SNS, MEAS, MON, FB) is taken as a signal without asking (/MCU/ADC_SENSE_1). A net already listed is selected.
  • The tick box at the start of a row switches the net on or off. Switched off, it keeps everything you entered (in the settings file as enabled: false) and shows greyed, but Run checks, Simulate and the fix search leave it out, and it is not checked against the board. The Converters and Bridges tabs have the same tick box, as do DC loads, Power integrity and Signal integrity's pairs. Select All, above the tick column of each of these tables, ticks every row, or unticks them when all were ticked; it shows a dash when only some are. With every row off, Simulate says so. The command line runs a switched-off row only when --nets names it.
  • The form on the right edits the selected row: Add (a new net from the form), Update selected (editing a detected net counts as reviewing it; the row stays selected for the next edit), Remove selected, Mark reviewed (the orange stops). Several rows at once: Ctrl- or Shift-click them; the form shows each signal field they share, and * where they differ, with each path's own net, driver and receivers greyed. Change a field (e.g. Amplitude to 5) and press Update selected: only the fields you changed are set on every selected path, a * left as it is keeps each path's own value. Hover over a field or a button for what it means. With no nets yet, a line above the table says how to start.
  • Clicking a row fills the form and highlights the net's whole path in KiCad, from its driver through its series parts, Pass through parts and op-amps to its receivers (Zoom KiCad to the selected nets also zooms to it; ~ in KiCad clears the highlight). Several rows can be selected (Ctrl / Shift-click, Ctrl+A), removed (Delete) or marked reviewed at once.
  • How nets are found at the bottom, Show / Hide: the net and pin names detection looks for (the text can be selected and copied).

Efficiency over load current

Every Simulate sweeps each power stage's load current and shows its efficiency and losses as a Results view of their own (<stage> efficiency), a part of the report and lines in the console output. No new solve is needed: the losses are the ones Thermal heats the parts with, worked out again at each current, and the copper of the stage's DC paths scales with the square of its current (one Simulate gives its resistance).

  • The plot: the efficiency against the load current above, the losses below on the same current axis, stacked by cause (conduction, the inductor's winding, the inductor's core, output charge, switching, dead time, reverse recovery, a diode, copper, series parts and connector pins, gate drive, the controller), the total as a line. The dotted line is the stage's own load current. The second plot shows the loss at that current by cause, with each one's share: the place to start when the efficiency should be higher.
  • Light load: the switches' conduction and the winding carry the inductor's ripple as well as the load (I² + ΔI²/12), and the gates' drive and the controller's own supply cost the same at every current, so the efficiency falls at light load as measured curves do. Below the shaded band's edge (I = ΔI/2) the inductor current reaches zero each cycle: a controller may skip pulses, burst or run discontinuous there and do better than the curve, which stays in continuous conduction.
  • What it needs (Converters tab): R_DS(on), the inductor's DCR and C_oss / Q_oss as for Thermal, and for the fixed losses Q_g (one FET's gate charge, its Qg field), Gate drive (V) (5 V when empty) and Controller I_q (its supply current while switching). A value not given is left out and the view's notes say so. The inductor's Core loss (Losses group) is the loss the maker's tool or curves give: alone, the loss at this stage's frequency and ripple; with Core loss at f and at ΔI (the ripple, peak to peak) a point of the maker's data, scaled by the Steinmetz equation P ∝ f^α·ΔI^β (α 1.3 and β 2.2 assumed when empty, with a note) and for the duty. The ripple does not follow the load, so the core loss is the same at every load current: it weighs most at light load. It also heats the inductor in Thermal. The inductor's schematic fields Core Loss, Core Loss Frequency, Core Loss Ripple, Core Alpha and Core Beta fill these (◆), as its DCR field does. With Core loss at ΔI the inductor needs its L on the Parts tab (the stage's ripple comes from it): without it the core loss is left out and a note says so; with only Core loss at f it is scaled by the frequency alone. In the settings file: core_loss, core_loss_f, core_loss_di, core_alpha, core_beta (0.5 to 4), and for the fixed losses qg, v_gate, iq. The winding's AC resistance and the capacitors' ESR loss from their ripple current are not modelled.
  • The currents: 24 points from 5 % to 120 % of the stage's load current, or the list in Efficiency currents (A) (settings: efficiency_currents: [0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6]).
  • Measured points: Measured efficiency names a CSV (a file in the board's groundtruth/ folder, or a full path) with the load current (A), the efficiency (% or 0-1) and optionally the loss (W) in its first three columns; a header row is skipped, commas or semicolons both work. Its points are drawn as open circles over the model's curves, and a tile gives the largest difference. The file is read on the computer that shows the result, so it also works with a compute server.

The hot loop

In a buck, the on-time loop (input capacitor, high-side switch, inductor, output capacitor) and the off-time loop (low-side switch, inductor, output capacitor) both carry trapezoidal currents: the inductor current never stops. The difference between them is the loop input capacitor → high-side switch → switch node → low-side switch → ground → input capacitor. It is the only loop with a fully switched current, from zero to the peak and back within each edge, so it holds the most high-frequency energy: the hot loop. A boost is a buck run backwards, its hot loop on the output; a 4-switch buck-boost has two, a flyback one on each winding.

It matters for two reasons. The loop radiates as a magnetic dipole whose field goes with the current switched times the area the loop covers. And its inductance stores energy at every edge that rings with the switches' C_oss: the overshoot, and the radiated peak at the ringing frequency.

How to keep it small:

  • the loop as small as possible, with the smallest copper spacing the design rules allow;
  • the shortest, flattest ceramic capacitor closes the loop first, right at the switches;
  • several capacitors in parallel are fine: the smallest package closest to the loop, the larger ones right behind it;
  • a solid ground plane on the layer directly under the loop, so the return runs under the forward path.

In GroundTruth: the loop inductance, ringing and overshoot of a stage's Results view are the numbers to compare before and after a change, and Draw on board ▾ › Hot loop draws on User.1 which capacitors actually carry the loop current at 100 MHz and in what share, with an outline around everything carrying 5 % or more. A large share through capacitors far from the switches (or through another stage's) means the loop closes the long way round: move the small capacitors next to the switches. Source: Analog Devices, Layout for Power Designs #1: Hot Loops (C. Kueck, 2018).

Converters tab

The switching power stages (DC-DC converters): name, topology, rail, switch node, ground, the switches (high side, low side, or an IC with its switches inside), switching frequency, input and output voltage, load current, edge time, and optionally C_oss / Q_oss, R_DS(on), the inductor's DCR, a diode's V_f, the stray capacitance to a chassis, the input and output connectors and the conducted standard. Hover over a field for what it means. Several stages at once: Ctrl- or Shift-click them; the form shows each field they share, and * where they differ (a list gets a * entry), with each stage's own name and switches greyed. Change a field (e.g. V in) and press Update selected: only the fields you changed are set on every selected stage, a * left as it is keeps each stage's own value. C_oss, Q_oss, R_DS(on), DCR and V_f come from the parts' schematic fields where they have them: ◆ beside a field, or ! where a value set here differs; Use schematic values takes them back (Part parameters from the schematic). Inductor DCR is also checked against the inductor's R / DCR on the Parts tab (one physical value): ◇ where they agree (or the field is empty and the losses take that R), ! where they differ. Click a !, or right-click its field, to reset that one field to its source's value (the schematic's, or the Parts tab's R): it is saved at once, and what is typed in the other fields stays. The controller's spread spectrum is on the form: Spread spectrum (%) (f_sw dithered up to that much above it; empty: off), Spread mode (random hops, or a triangle sweep) and Spread rate (the triangle's modulation frequency; greyed for random). random is the pseudo-random dither most controllers use (the LTC7890's too): a new frequency every switching cycle, no rate needed (a settings file may still give spread_rate as hops per second; the form's Update saves none for random). A triangle needs its modulation frequency from the controller's datasheet: how fast the sweep runs against the EMI receiver's bandwidth (9 kHz from 150 kHz to 30 MHz) decides how much the reading drops (a slow sweep hardly lowers it, a fast one by several dB), so there is no safe default; the field's grey hint says so when triangle is chosen. Q_g, Gate drive (V), Controller I_q (A), Efficiency currents (A) and Measured efficiency feed the efficiency sweep (Efficiency over load current). Interleave group and Phase (°) mark the phases of one controller (Interleaved stages below). The buttons are those of the Signal paths tab, and the rows show in KiCad the same way: clicking a row selects the stage's switches and hot-loop capacitors and highlights its switch node (Zoom KiCad to the selected power stages also zooms). KiCad shows the row you stop on: clicking or arrowing quickly through the rows does not make it select and zoom to each one.

The table's columns: Name, Status (as on the Signal paths tab), Topology, Switches (high side / low side, or "integrated" and the IC), Rail, f_sw, I out (the load current: the output's; the input current is worked out, see below), V in→out (? where not set), Edge, Input and Output (the connector pins). The form scrolls when the window is short. Dead time, reverse conduction and recovery (a synchronous stage: a FET as the rectifier, a buck's low side or a boost's high side). Before each switch turns on, both are off for a dead time; the inductor current meanwhile flows through the rectifier FET in reverse, at Reverse V_SD (about 1.5-2 V for GaN, 0.8 V for a silicon body diode) instead of across its R_DS(on). Each edge of the switch node has its own: Dead time, rise (low side off → high side on; the LTC7890's DTCA) and Dead time, fall (high side off → low side on; its DTCB). At the main switch's turn-on (a buck's rise, the hard edge) the whole dead time is reverse conduction, at the ripple's valley current. At its turn-off (a buck's fall, the soft edge) the inductor current, at its peak, first swings the node across, moving both FETs' output charge (2·Q_oss, else 2·C_oss·V): only the rest of that dead time is reverse conduction. The rectifier's channel is off for both. With GaN the loss is often as much as the FET's conduction (refboard's conv1 at 10 A with 2 × 20 ns: 0.25 W, against 0.15 W of conduction; the fall's 7 ns node swing takes 0.07 W off). The dead time is not the edge time: the edge is how fast the node swings once a switch turns on. Set them here, or give the controller IC a GT field (Detection labels): dead:20ns for both edges of every converter it controls, dead1:20ns / dead2:30ns per channel (its SW1 / SW2 pin), conv1_dead:20ns for the converter labelled conv1, and any of them for one edge: dead_rise:20ns, dead1_fall:10ns, conv1_dead_rise:15ns (for each edge the most specific word wins). The values are then marked ◆ like the parts' fields. Empty: 20 ns (an adaptive controller's; the LTC7890 with its DTCA / DTCB pins on GND). V_SD comes from the rectifier FETs' Vsd field, else 2 V for a GaN part and 0.8 V otherwise. A silicon FET's body diode also has a reverse-recovery charge Q_rr (its Qrr field; GaN has none, leave it empty): the main switch sweeps it out at its hard turn-on (Q_rr·V·f_sw of loss), the hot loop carries it as a current spike of I_rr = √(2·Q_rr·di/dt) (in the conducted and radiated spectra), and when the recovery snaps off it adds an overshoot I_rr·√(L/C_oss), shown on the Results tab's recovery tile and in the hot_loop issue. Thermal's notes say when a default was used.

Each member stage shows the group's conducted and radiated margin together, the same sum in phase for comparison, and each stage's own margin. A group below the EMI margin has its own issue (interleave group …); the stages' own issues stay, they are what each stage would do alone. With two or more stages the Results tab's Route list starts with all power stages DC currents together: every stage at its own load at once, their currents added (they share the input connector, a surge stopper, the rail and the ground). Its title gives the loads, the total input current and the copper's loss of the total current. The current-density and via-load checks run on this total, as issues of all power stages; the stages' own DC views still show what each stage alone puts where. The switch node carries the current from the high side for the duty D and from the low side for the rest: its densities, via currents and copper loss are the RMS of the two (the arrows show the mean flow), and Thermal heats the copper with that. A part's current enters the copper at its pins by each pin's copper area, spread evenly over it; where a footprint draws one pin from overlapping pads (an EPC GaN FET's source pin: a bar under three tabs) that copper counts once. A pin without vias of its own sends its share through the nearest vias around it, which then carry the most.
  • The input current is not entered: it follows from the energy balance V_in·I_in = V_out·I_out + losses (a buck at 100 % efficiency would draw I_out·D). The losses are the ones Thermal uses: the switches' conduction (R_DS(on), with the inductor's ripple: I² + ΔI²/12), the hard edge (½·V·I·t_r·f_sw at the inductor current of that instant: a synchronous stage's turn-on at the ripple's valley, a diode stage's turn-off at its peak too) and output charge (Q_oss·V·f_sw; a diode stage half of it), a synchronous rectifier's reverse conduction in the dead times and its recovery charge (Q_rr·V·f_sw), a diode's V_f, the inductor's DCR and its core loss, series parts, connector pins (contact and solder joint) and the copper of the DC paths, and the gate drive and the controller's own supply when Q_g and I_q are given (the same losses as the efficiency view's at this current); a value not given (no R_DS(on), no DCR) counts as no loss: the stage's notes say which. The stage's line on the Results tab shows the input current and efficiency, its notes the loss break-down, and the DC input path's map (connector to the high side) carries this current: its title reads "expected input current … A (efficiency … %)" beside the path's resistance and voltage drop. The ground return splits by it too: the input connector's minus pin takes the input current back, the low side the rest.
  • The radiated estimate counts the charge each edge moves into the switch node (C_oss, or Q_oss where given) on top of the load current: near the ringing it can be several times the load pulse (a light load on a high input voltage). Without C_oss there is neither ringing nor that current: for a diode buck give the diode's junction capacitance as C_oss. The estimate is the hot loop's own radiation; cables usually radiate more, so read it as a lower bound.
  • Ceramic input capacitors lose much of their capacitance at a high DC voltage (an X7R keeps roughly 30-60 % at half its rating). A stage's notes name those still at their nominal value: give them a DC-bias curve (Cbias, see Cbias: capacitance at DC bias); each then gets its capacitance at the stage's input voltage.

Bridges tab

Motor bridges: half-bridge legs on one bus, the motor's winding as the inductor (BLDC drives, ESCs, starter-generators). The legs, their phase nodes, drivers, snubbers and the motor connector are detected; the operating point and the motor are yours to enter:

Synchronous: the chopping leg's other switch conducts in the off time (complementary PWM with dead time). A scope on a leg's two gates shows which.

The legs of the bridge in the form are listed under the bridges (Legs of the bridge in the form: phase, switch node, high side, low side, output pin, driver and the nets it is driven from). Clicking a leg fills the Selected leg fields (Phase, Switch node, High side, Low side, Output pin); Set leg replaces the selected leg with them, Add leg adds one, Remove leg removes the selected one. Per-phase current sensing. A leg's low side may sit straight on the ground, or on a net of its own that reaches the ground through a current-sense shunt (one per phase, e.g. /Phase A/PHASE_GND). Detection takes such a net as ground when the parts between it and a ground net pass current: 0 Ω links, ferrites, 4-terminal (Kelvin) shunts (through their two large force pads; the small sense pads carry no current), current-sense ICs and resistors up to 10 Ω, at most two in series (a larger resistor is a load, not a shunt). The legs are then one bridge on the common ground, each with its own net and shunt as its Return (the leg list's column; Ground return and Shunt in the Selected leg fields); legs with and without shunts mix. Simulate closes each leg's hot loop through its shunt: the shunt and its copper are in the loop, its RMS current and loss in the bridge's results (a low-side shunt carries its leg's chopped current as well as the slow phase current: its RMS and loss count both). When detection still splits a drive into several bridges (a shunt above 10 Ω, a return net it cannot follow), tick one of them in Detect…, then add the other legs to it with Add leg (their switch node, high and low side, ground return, shunt and output pin) and set the common Ground net; or label the switches hbA_hs, hbA_ls, … (Detection labels).

Simulate then, per bridge:

  • drives the motor in six-step at the operating point and finds the duty for the phase current (an issue says when the bus cannot drive it at that speed);
  • solves each leg's hot loop: inductance, L·di/dt, ringing (with the leg's snubber), radiated estimate. A leg that does not switch at the operating point (a locked rotor drives one commutation step) gets no hot-loop checks;
  • adds the legs at the input: the conducted emission at the battery, read by a simulated EMI receiver against the limit;
  • shares the ripple among the bus capacitors: the legs' bus-side currents added in time, each by the capacitor's share of that leg's switching current at f_pwm. Capacitors that share every leg alike carry their share of the bus current's AC part; a leg's 120° conduction block cancels in the bus and is no ripple;
  • maps the RMS currents from the battery to the motor (phase currents, bus current, switching currents) with the busiest vias against their IPC-2221 rating;
  • counts the parts' losses: each FET's conduction, reverse conduction in the dead times, hard turn-on and output charge; the snubber resistors; the capacitors' ESR; the series parts on the battery path (a part modelled as a short gets no loss: give it a resistor model);
  • checks the victims (see above) and runs the sweep.

Victims… (under the legs table; the line beside it says what is set) picks the nets the switching may upset, for every bridge and power stage. On the left the board's nets (type to filter, Ctrl- or Shift-click to select several), on the right the victims with why each is one. Add → adds the nets selected on the left (several at once, or double-click one), ← Remove removes those selected on the right (or Delete, or double-click). On the Results tab (the view list at its top) a bridge has four kinds of view, in this order:

  • the bridge by its name: its drive (phase and bus currents, capacitor ripple, conducted spectrum, losses);
  • each leg's hot loop (listed by its switches, Q1+Q2→Q3+Q4): the leg's commutation loop at 100 MHz;
  • bridge RMS currents: what heats the copper, from the battery to the motor connector;
  • bridge sweep (when a sweep is set): losses, RMS density, conducted and radiated margin against the phase current, one line per speed; a hollow marker where the current is not reached.

The sweep (the form's Sweep currents and Sweep speeds, optional) is in addition to the operating point set above it (Phase current, Speed), not instead of it. The operating point is what Simulate solves and checks: its hot loops, maps, RMS currents and issues. The sweep then takes every sweep current at every sweep speed (25, 50, 100 A × 0, 1000 rpm: six points; an empty list uses the operating point's value) and re-uses the operating point's solves for each, since the copper is linear: the drive again, the RMS currents re-weighted, the capacitors' ripple, the losses and the conducted reading. The hot loop's L·di/dt and radiated margin are scaled with the largest switched current. That takes seconds per point, no new solves. Its points raise no issues; they are shown in the bridge's sweep view on the Results tab, one line per speed against the phase current, with a hollow marker where the drive cannot reach the current (the bus voltage against the back-EMF at that speed). Use it to see how losses and margins move over the drive's range; set the operating point to the case you want checked.

Why the hot loop does not reach the motor connector. During a switching edge the motor's winding holds the phase current nearly constant: the current only moves from one switch to the other. What changes in nanoseconds flows around the bus capacitors, the high side, the phase node and the low side, and back to the capacitors. That is the hot loop, and its map stops at the phase node. The motoring current itself (battery, high side, phase node, motor pin and back through the low side) changes slowly. It is in the RMS currents map, which is made from DC solves plus the first three PWM harmonics, where current spreads by resistance, not inductance. If that view is missing, a leg has no Output pin (the run's notes say so). What does leave through the motor connector at high frequency is common-mode current: the phase node's swing drives it through the cable's and the motor frame's capacitance to the chassis. It is estimated as a single figure from the bridge's C stray (chassis), not mapped.

Motor or generator. For the same reason, the machine on the phase output is not part of the loop inductance: through an edge it is a constant current at the phase node. What it changes is the current that is switched (the overshoot grows with it) and which edge is the hard one: motoring, the current flows out of the phase node and the high side's turn-on is hard; generating (the bridge's Mode), it flows in and the low side's turn-on is. The loop and its inductance are the same in both.

What the loop inductance covers. It is the inductance of the leg's commutation loop on this board: the bus capacitors between the rail and the ground within 20 mm of the switches (or, when the nearest one is farther, those within 5 mm beyond it), the high- and low-side switches, the copper of the rail, phase node and ground nets with their vias and pours on every layer, and the capacitors' own ESL. A switch's current runs 0.25 mm above its pads, inside its package [A30]; what lies deeper in the die and the bond is not included. The model region reaches 5 mm around the switches, the capacitors and the input path, and widens to 10 and 20 mm when the loop does not close within it. The loop is driven at the switches and its reactance solved at 30 and 100 MHz; a series LC through both, X = ωL − 1/(ωC), leaves out what is left of the capacitors' reactance. The battery and its leads are beyond the bus capacitors and play no part at these frequencies. The hot_loop issue names the two closest capacitors.

Higher or lower loop inductance. A tight layout with ceramic capacitors right at the switches and the return on the layer underneath reaches a few nH; a loop that closes through distant or electrolytic capacitors 10-20 nH and more. A higher inductance means:

  • more overshoot on the phase node, L·di/dt: the hot_loop issue warns at 25 % of the bus voltage and is an error at 50 % (rules.overshoot_warning, overshoot_error). It eats the switches' voltage margin;
  • more ringing energy, ½·L·I² at every edge, lost in the switches, the snubber or the copper;
  • a lower ring frequency, 1/(2π·√(L·Coss)), with a peak in the conducted and radiated spectrum there;
  • more radiation: a larger loop area is a larger magnetic moment.

A lower inductance does the opposite: less overshoot and ringing energy, a higher ring frequency, a smaller loop. To lower it: the capacitors closer to the switches, the return on the next layer down through a thin dielectric, more vias in parallel, and more capacitors in parallel.

Why 100 MHz. The spectrum and the conducted emission are solved over their whole range; only the hot-loop map is drawn at one frequency. 100 MHz is chosen because:

  • the edges and the ringing are up there: an edge of rise time tr has a flat spectrum up to about 1/(π·tr), then it falls at 40 dB per decade (16 MHz for 20 ns, 64 MHz for 5 ns). The loop's inductance rings with the switches' Coss, typically at 30-300 MHz: the overshoot on the phase node and a peak in the spectrum;
  • the limits go there: conducted emission to 30 MHz (CISPR 25: 108 MHz), radiated from 30 MHz;
  • the loop inductance is measured there: every capacitor in the loop is far above its self-resonance, so the loop's impedance is nearly pure inductance (fitted with a second solve at 30 MHz). That L sets the overshoot (L·di/dt), the ringing and the radiated estimate;
  • the picture holds for the whole band: above about 10 MHz the current already takes the path of least inductance (the return right under the forward path), so a map at 20 or 50 MHz looks the same. It changes at a plane resonance, which an issue names with its frequency.

Drives with deliberately slow edges (50-200 ns) put little energy at 100 MHz: there the map shows where the ringing current flows rather than where most of the emitted energy is.

DC loads tab

A DC load is a supply rail with the currents its loads draw: a power connector feeding several boards' connectors, a pour or track to a regulator, a fused output. Simulate solves each rail on the copper of the whole board (pours, tracks and vias, as for a power stage's DC paths) and shows:

  • the voltage drop each load sees: on the supply side (from the source pin to the load's pin) and, when the return pins are given, on the ground side (back to the source's return pin), and the total;
  • the current density with its flow (a view of its own on the Results tab), the hottest narrow spot and the busiest vias against IPC-2221, and the copper's loss; these are checked like a power stage's DC paths (current_density, via_overload), and a load above the rail's Max drop is an issue (dc_drop). The checked density is the one sustained over 1 mm of the rail's own copper (another net's pour beside a track does not dilute it); its limits are rules of thumb (50 A/mm² a warning, 100 an error; a via from 100 % and 150 % of its IPC-2221 capacity: rules), and what a density does to the copper's temperature is Thermal's. The peak shown beside it is the density over a quarter millimetre of copper (or one cell, where the cell is larger): at an inside corner the density has no finite value at a point, so a single cell's figure would grow on every finer grid; over 0.25 mm it settles (a coarse grid reads it up to about 10 % low);
  • Thermal heats the board with it, and Current capacity finds the current at which the hottest copper or part reaches the limit, all the rail's loads scaled together.

Each load draws its current whatever the drop (a current sink), and the source pin is the reference (an ideal source). So narrowing a track to a quarter of its width gives that track four times the resistance, drop, loss and current density at the same current. The drops are for copper at 20 °C: about 0.4 % more per kelvin the board runs warmer; after Thermal, its notes (and the JSON report's dc_at_temperature) give each DC path's resistance, drop and loss at the temperature the solve found along it. Copper of the rail's net that does not touch (a track broken by a gap) is not joined, however narrow the gap: the rail is then reported as not connected.

Fill from net… is the quick way: pick the rail's net (e.g. VDD) and its ground (GND); GroundTruth lists every part with a pin on the rail, with that part's own ground pin, and you pick the part feeding it (the connector with the fewest pins is preselected) and the parts drawing from it (the connectors are preselected), then a current per load. The form is filled in; check it, change a line's current if a load draws more, and press Add.

Typing pins: the Source pin, Source return and Loads fields list the board's pins while you type, each with its net and part (Ctrl+Space, or Down in a one-line field, lists them on an empty field); Up/Down choose, Enter or Tab takes one, Escape closes the list. Typing matches a pin's start (j3: J3's pins) or, from two letters, its net (vdd). The order follows the field: the source offers supply connectors first, the return the source part's own ground pin first; in Loads, a line's pin comes from the source pin's net (pins already listed are left out), followed by a "/" and its own part's pin on the return's net, then "=" for the current.

The form: Name; Source pin, the pin the rail is fed from (REF.PAD, e.g. J1.2); Source return, optional (e.g. J1.1, GND); Loads, one per line as PIN/RETURN=CURRENT, e.g. J2.2/J2.1=0.5A (without returns: J2.2=0.5A; give the returns for every load and the source, or for none); Max drop, optional, in volts. Every load draws its current at once (the solve is linear: one load alone is not needed to know its share). The tick box at the start of a row switches it off without losing it. With power stages on the same copper, the DC currents together view adds the rails too. The model's cells start at the board's dielectric height: a pour reads a few % low at 1x resolution (0.24 mm cells widen its edges); 4x is converged.

Power integrity tab (PDN impedance)

For a supply rail, the impedance its ICs see between their supply and ground pins, from 10 kHz to 1 GHz: the decaps (their C, ESR and ESL from the Parts tab, plus their pads and vias), the planes' capacitance, the copper and the regulator together, solved on one model of the whole board. Each IC with supply pins (schematic pin type power_in) on the rail and a pad on its ground is a port: a current step at its pins and the voltage it makes there. The rail holds its voltage within the allowed ripple when the impedance stays below the target V · ripple / I_step, whatever the frequency of the IC's current step.

  • per IC the curve |Z(f)| with its phase below it (−90°: capacitive, +90°: inductive, crossing 0° at a resonance; the HTML report's Power integrity part and the rail's view), its peaks (anti-resonances: two decaps, or the decaps and the planes, ringing together), the part holding the rail at each frequency (the regulator, a decap, the planes) and the inductance above its decaps;
  • issues: pdn_impedance where an IC's curve comes within 3 dB of the target (a warning; over the target an error) below check_to (default 100 MHz: above it the package and the die's own capacitance take over), naming the part holding the rail there; decap_missing for supply pins with no decap pad within decap_mm (3 mm);
  • the source (the regulator's output, found by its power_out pin, or a connector) is modelled as vrm_r + jωvrm_l (5 mΩ, 20 nH unless set): below the decaps' range the rail is the regulator's.

The model is the whole board at the board-wide resolution (Preferences), and each IC's pins with their nearby decaps on a fine model of their own: the inductance at small pads and vias, which the board-wide grid cannot resolve, is added to the IC's curve. An IC whose small pads the board-wide grid cannot join to the rail's copper is left out with a note (raise the resolution).

The Power integrity tab lists the rails: Find rails adds every rail ICs draw supply from (its source found by itself); set each one's Load step for a target (and its voltage when the name has none). After Simulate, each rail is a view on the Results tab: the curves with the target, and under them what holds the rail at each IC across frequency. In the settings file:

pdn:
  - {rail: +3V3, i_step: 300mA}
  - {rail: VDD_CORE, voltage: 1.2V, ripple: 3%, i_step: 2A, source: U7, vrm_r: 2mΩ, vrm_l: 10nH}

Signal integrity tab

The characteristic impedance of the listed nets along their tracks, differential pairs, crosstalk and via stubs, from cross-sections of the board: a cut across every copper layer perpendicular to the track, every 2 mm, with all the copper in it (other nets, pours and planes as the reference at 0 V), the stackup's dielectrics and the solder mask, solved as a two-dimensional field problem (within 0.2 % of exact results). Run checks does it: a few seconds per net; the figures show on this tab and in the report, the issues on the Issues tab.

  • impedance: Z0 along a net's tracks outside its target. Targets on this tab (Impedance target: nets by name or pattern such as /MCU/SPI_*, or a KiCad net class, with Z0 and tolerance). A listed net without a target gets its Z0 shown. A void in the plane under a track shows as a higher Z0 there.
  • diff_impedance, diff_skew: a pair's differential impedance where its two nets run side by side (elsewhere it is uncoupled, about twice Z0), and the delay mismatch of their routed lengths against Max skew (by default a tenth of the pair's edge). Find pairs lists the pairs found by name (_P/_N, +/-, CAN_H/CAN_L, DP/DM, X and X*), with the interface's impedance where the name tells it (USB 90 Ω, Ethernet, LVDS, HDMI 100 Ω); CAN and RS-485 get none (their slow edges need none on the board).
  • crosstalk: a listed net (its edge and swing from the Signal paths tab) running beside another track on one layer: near-end and far-end crosstalk as a share of its swing, from the coupled length, the spacing and the edge; above the limit (5 %) an issue on the victim.

Through-hole pins (a connector, a big inductor's leads): how a part's pins conduct through the board and join it. Built in, a soldered pin is copper filling the whole drilled hole, joined to every layer around its ring, with nothing in series: a perfect joint. Where that is too kind, give the part its values, comma separated, e.g. contact:0.5m, fill:75, pin:brass:

  • joint: the solder joint's resistance per pin;
  • fill: how far up the hole the solder goes, in % of the board's thickness from the soldering side (the side opposite the part). 100 built in; IPC-A-610 accepts 75. Above the solder only the hole's thin plating joins the layers to the pin, and the part's current enters the board where the solder ends;
  • pin: the pin's conductor: hole (built in: copper filling the hole), copper, brass, bronze (phosphor bronze), becu or tellurium. With a material the pin is a rod of pin_d (mm; default the hole less 0.25 mm), the gap is solder and the plating conducts alongside; pin_r gives the pin's own resistance through the board instead.

No contact value is guessed: a connector pin carrying 3 A or more without one gets a note. fill and pin change the hole's conduction between layers in every analysis.

Cbias: capacitance at DC bias

What it is. A class II ceramic capacitor (X7R, X5R, X6S, Y5V …) loses capacitance as the DC voltage across it rises: its barium-titanate dielectric's permittivity falls with the field. A 100 nF 100 V X7R 0603 keeps about 80 % at 20 V, half at 40 V and 15 % at 100 V; small packages and low-voltage parts lose more. The maker's datasheet shows it as a DC Bias Characteristics chart (ΔC % against DC volts). Class I ceramics (C0G / NP0), tantalum and aluminium capacitors don't lose capacitance this way.

Why per capacitor. One part is used in many places: refboard's 100 nF 100 V X7R sits on the 55 V input (~70 nF there) and at the 3.3 V ADC input (~100 nF). A single "capacitance at bias" is right for one of them only, so the part carries its curve, and every copy gets its capacitance at its own DC voltage.

How to give it. On the symbol (from the HTTP lib) or in the Parts tab's C at bias cell, 1 to 12 points (at most 12: more add nothing a curve read by eye can give):

Conducted-emission limits: standards and classes

Every power stage and motor bridge with an input connector gets a conducted-emission estimate: the voltage a test receiver would read across the LISN (line impedance stabilisation network) at its input, per harmonic, against a limit line. The Conducted standard and Conducted class on the Converters and Bridges tabs pick that line, as TI WEBENCH's Noise standard and Noise class do: CISPR 22 with CLASS A / CLASS B, CISPR 25 with CLASS 1 to CLASS 5, and besides those CISPR 32 (the default) and MIL-STD-461 CE102. A class is the same measurement with a different allowed level: which one applies is set by where the product is sold or by the customer's specification, not by the design.

Which class a stage is checked against. Its own class when one is chosen. (default): with the analysis' standard ((analysis default), or the same standard chosen) the settings' analysis.conducted_class; with another standard that standard's strictest class (CLASS B, CLASS 5, the basic CE102 curve). Hovering over the standard shows what (analysis default) is now. In the settings file: conducted and conducted_class per stage or bridge, analysis.conducted and analysis.conducted_class for all; a class the standard doesn't have is an error that lists the valid ones.

Solid and dashed lines. GroundTruth compares with the average limit (solid). A switching converter's harmonics are steady lines in the spectrum, and a steady line reads the same on the peak, quasi-peak and average detectors, so the lowest limit (the average one) decides [CISPR 16-1-1]. Datasheets and WEBENCH draw the quasi-peak line for CISPR 22 and the peak line for CISPR 25 (dashed): 10-13 dB (CISPR 22) and 20 dB (CISPR 25) above the average ones. A margin in GroundTruth is therefore smaller than the same spectrum's margin in WEBENCH by about that much. Spread spectrum is the exception: it smears each harmonic over the receiver's bandwidth and lowers the average reading more than the peak, which Simulate models when the stage has a spread. The quasi-peak (CISPR 22 / 32) or peak (CISPR 25) limit can then be the one that binds, above all when the dither is slower than the receiver's 9 kHz bandwidth: Simulate reads both detectors and keeps the smaller margin (a stage's note says when the second limit binds; its rows then show that limit and a peak reading).

CISPR 22 (and CISPR 32): CLASS A and CLASS B. Information technology and multimedia equipment on the mains or a DC network, 150 kHz to 30 MHz, LISN 50 Ω ∥ (50 µH + 5 Ω) [CISPR 16-1-2]. CISPR 32 replaced CISPR 22 in 2017 and kept its conducted limits, so the two lines are the same; choose the one your test plan names [CISPR 22] [CISPR 32, Annex A].

  • CLASS B: residential (homes, offices next to homes; anything sold to consumers). 56 dBµV at 150 kHz falling to 46 dBµV at 500 kHz, 46 to 5 MHz, 50 to 30 MHz (average). The strictest: 10-14 dB below class A.
  • CLASS A: commercial and industrial equipment not meant for homes (it carries a warning that it may cause interference there). 66 dBµV to 500 kHz, then 60 (average).
@LIMITS_CISPR22@

CISPR 25: CLASS 1 to CLASS 5. Electronics in vehicles, boats and machines with engines, measured on the bench with a 5 µH artificial network [CISPR 25]. The limits protect the vehicle's own radio receivers, so they only exist in the broadcast bands (LW, MW, SW, CB, VHF, TV band I, FM); between the bands there is no limit and the chart shows none (WEBENCH draws a line across the gaps). Each class is a step looser than the next: 10 dB per class in LW, 8 dB in MW, 6 dB above [A10].

  • CLASS 5: the strictest; the usual demand for modules near the antenna or the radio (infotainment, cameras and ADAS, body electronics in the cabin), and what many vehicle makers ask for by default.
  • CLASS 3 and 4: modules further from the receivers or behind metal, or bands the vehicle has no receiver for; the maker's specification often names a class per band (class 5 for AM / FM, class 3 elsewhere, for example).
  • CLASS 1 and 2: the loosest; rarely asked for (engine bay, trucks and machines without a radio).

The 5 µH network has a lower impedance than CISPR 22's 50 µH below a few MHz, so the same converter reads lower on it there: dBµV on the two standards cannot be compared one to one.

Fixes tab

Suggest fixes proposes changes for the issues found, each placed where it would be legal on the board, predicted on the solver's model and verified together by simulating again:

  • signal nets: a stitching via at a layer change, a capacitor between two nets at a layer change or across a split, a fill that closes a slot; advice where no part helps;

Right-click a change for a menu: the schematic in a window of its own (Copy puts the drawing on the clipboard as an image, Save as PNG… writes it), the schematic copied to the clipboard, the parts copied to the clipboard, Check in Simulate, this help. The buttons beside the schematic do the same. The schematic is an example of the topology with the advisor's values, not a footprint-level design: rate the parts for the stage's voltage and current. They are worked out on the stage's own solves (the loop, its moment and its input path stay), in moments:

  • RC snubber across the switch node (C a few times C_oss, R about √(L/C_oss)): it damps the ringing; its resistor burns C·V²·f_sw, so the list gives the best-damping one and the lowest-loss one that meets the overshoot target.

Thermal and Current capacity

Thermal (Results tab, Further analysis ▾, after Simulate) solves the copper and part temperatures with the heat of every converter, every motor bridge and every DC load from the last Simulate (a DC load's current capacity scales all its loads together). It reads the board first: if the board or the settings changed since that Simulate, it says so and asks for a Simulate instead of heating the design as it was:

  • copper: a converter's DC paths, a bridge's RMS currents (Joule heat, resistivity following the temperature);
  • parts: their losses at the operating point (switch conduction, switching edges, output charge, a synchronous rectifier's reverse conduction in the dead times and its recovery charge, an inductor's DCR and core loss, series parts, connector pins' contact and joint resistance, and the gate drive and the controller's supply as heat in the controller IC (when Q_g and I_q are given). A FET's conduction loss rises with its temperature (0.5 %/K from 25 °C; rds_on_tc in the stage's settings sets another value, 0 none) and a winding's DCR loss with copper's 0.39 %/K, in Thermal and Current capacity; a part in several stages' input paths (the input pass FET) is heated by their summed current; for a bridge, the losses from its drive waveform).

Current capacity (Results tab, Further analysis ▾) finds, per converter, the load current, and per bridge, the phase current, at which the hottest copper or part junction reaches the limit (thermal.capacity_c, by default max_c; Preferences, Capacity limit, sets one for this computer), the others at their operating points. When the other stages at their operating points already put a spot over the limit with this one at no load, its capacity reads blocked and names the stage whose part is too hot (for example "buck conv1 at its operating point already puts copper at Q41 at 143.7 °C"): fix that stage's heat first; its own capacity view shows it below its operating point. The thermal settings: ambient temperature, orientation, natural or forced convection (air speed), the warning and maximum temperatures, and the parts' junction-to-board resistances (theta_jb). In the settings file, under thermal:: ambient_c 25, orientation horizontal or vertical, convection natural, forced (airspeed 1 m/s) or fixed (h_top 10 and h_bottom 5 W/m²K), radiation true, warn_c 85, max_c 105, theta_jb: {U1: 12}. The thermal mesh is the settings' thermal.quality (draft, normal, fine) unless Preferences sets one for this computer. Both run in the background; Cancel stops them. The solve is steady state: a load that lasts a few seconds (a cranking pulse) heats less than shown.

Thermal adds a temperature view to the Results tab's View list (replacing an earlier one), its issues join the Issues tab, and the status line gives the hottest copper and its layer and the power in copper and parts. The map shows each of its cells at the hottest point the solve found in it, so a track narrower than a map cell shows its own temperature. The copper's heat is exactly the DC paths' I²·R, its resistivity following the temperature, in the copper itself (also where a grid cell at the copper's edge has its point just outside it); a converter adds its inductor's ripple, the switching current its input capacitors send around the hot loop, and the heat in every via barrel the current passes. One convection coefficient per face, from the face's mean temperature (near a hot spot the real one is a little higher, so the estimate is on the warm side). The DC tables stay at 20 °C; Thermal's notes give each DC path at its own temperature (its copper's resistance, drop and loss). A long, narrow, hot track can use up the normal quality's mesh budget part-way along it (a note says so): the rest of it reads a few per cent cool there, and a track narrower than the finest cell (0.05 mm on normal) a few per cent cool too; Thermal Quality fine resolves both.

Electro-thermal (off by default; Preferences or the settings' thermal.electrothermal): hot copper is more resistive (0.39 % per kelvin), so a hot via or track hands current to cooler ones beside it. Off, the DC paths keep their 20 °C currents and only the heat follows the temperature. On, after the first thermal solve every converter's and DC load's DC paths are solved again with each copper cell and via barrel at its own temperature, and Thermal again with those currents, round after round until no path's loss and no via's share of its path's current moves by more than the tolerance (Settled within, 1 %), and never more than Rounds at most (3; 10 at the very most, whatever is set), so it cannot run on. A round that changes more than the one before ends it with the round before kept; Cancel stops it between solves. Copper's resistance rising with temperature makes the rounds converge: one or two usually settle it. The result's notes and the electro-thermal tile say whether it settled, in how many rounds, and how the busiest via's current moved (at 20 °C → at temperature); the report lists the busiest vias both ways. Each round costs one DC solve and one thermal solve; with a compute server the whole coupling, rounds and all, is one job there. Simulate's own DC tables stay at 20 °C. Bridges' RMS currents are not re-solved, and Current capacity keeps the 20 °C current pattern (only the heat follows the temperature there).

Current capacity adds one view per stage or bridge at its capacity (replacing earlier capacity views), and the status line lists each one's capacity and what limits it.

Op-amp circuits and the transfer function

A signal path is solved as the circuit it is on the board: every part with a model and every op-amp channel on its nets and on the nets beyond them, up to ground and the supply rails (a net with a supply pin, or named like a rail unless only resistors are on it: a divider's tap such as VREF_MID is a node of the circuit), whatever the topology: inverting, non-inverting, differential and summing stages, integrators, transimpedance stages, Sallen-Key, multiple-feedback and state-variable filters, instrumentation amplifiers, several op-amps with feedback between them. The parts as modelled on the Parts tab (a capacitor with its ESR, ESL and DC-bias capacitance), every track as a transmission line of its own (its width, layer and the stackup), every via with its resistance and inductance; a net the copper does not join (unrouted) is joined as the netlist says, with a note. The source is the driver behind its Source R, L and C; the load each receiver's capacitance and the Load R.

An op-amp is its datasheet's small-signal model: open-loop gain Aol (default 100 dB) with its pole at GBW/Aol (GBW 1 MHz when not given, with a note), an optional second pole (Fp2, or from the phase margin PM at unity gain), the open-loop output resistance Zout (the datasheet's R_O, default 100 Ω), each input's capacitance to ground (Cin, 2 pF) and Rin between the inputs; PM sets the second pole at GBW·sin(PM)·tan(PM), which gives a follower exactly that margin. Its gain, bandwidth and output impedance in the circuit then follow from the resistors and capacitors around it; a part with a Gain field stays a fixed gain block (for an amplifier GroundTruth can't see inside, such as an instrumentation-amplifier IC), as does an op-amp whose inverting input can't be told. The pins are found by their schematic names (+IN, IN+, INP, +, −IN, IN−, INN, −, OUT, VOUT, and per channel of a dual or quad: +INA, −INB, OUTA, IN1+ …; a single op-amp's unnamed output: its one pin of type output), else by the GT labels amp_in, amp_inv (the inverting input) and amp_out. Small signal only: no output swing, saturation or slew in the sweep.

How an op-amp is recognised. A part is an op-amp (the amplifier model on the Parts tab) when one of these holds, checked in this order:

  1. its symbol has a Gain field (it is then a fixed gain block);
  2. it has the GT labels amp_in / amp_out (and amp_inv where the inverting input's pin name doesn't say it);
  3. its pin names say it: exactly one non-inverting input (+, +IN, IN+, INP), one inverting input (−, −IN, IN−, INN), one output (OUT, VOUT, VO, or else its only pin of electrical type output) and supply pins (V+, V−, VCC, VDD, VEE, VSS). The supply pins are what tell it from a connector with + / − pins.

Detection runs a path through a recognised op-amp without labels: from the net of its input to the net of its output, the end on the input side as the driver (a connector feeding the stage), the end after it as the receiver. When the + input is on ground (an inverting stage, a multiple-feedback filter) the signal is taken in at the − input. The path's description lists the feedback parts (C5 1n0 ∥ U1, R4 2k94 → …).

A part marked DNP is never an op-amp. Anything else stays open: its pins are only ports, the circuit leaves it out, and the parts around it then behave as if it weren't there (a feedback network does nothing, the transfer function misses half the stage). The Signal paths tab warns about it (⚠ "… looks like an op-amp … but has no model") when the pin names point to an op-amp that is still open.

If an op-amp is not recognised (pins named differently, such as IN_A / FB / VOUT_SENSE, a dual or quad whose channels can't be told, an output pin of type passive), do one of these:

  • on the Parts tab, or in the settings file, set its model to amplifier; for a dual or quad also its stage's pins (input, then output);
  • or give its symbol the GT labels, e.g. GT = amp_in:3, amp_out:1 (add amp_inv:4 when the inverting input isn't named − / IN−; the second unit of a dual: amp_in:5, amp_out:7; several units: one group each, see Several channels of one op-amp), then Reload board: see GT labels;
  • or, for an amplifier IC whose inside doesn't matter (an instrumentation amplifier), a Gain field.

Logic buffers (Schmitt triggers). A Hall sensor's or a switch's input often runs through an RC filter into a Schmitt-trigger buffer (an SN74LVC2G17) and on to the microcontroller. GroundTruth takes that as one path through the buffer (the part model buffer): the stretch before it ends at the buffer's input, loaded by its input capacitance; the stretch after it is driven by the buffer's own edge (the path's frequency and duty, the buffer's rise time, swinging its supply pin's voltage) behind its output resistance. The buffer does not pass the input's waveform: a slow, filtered edge in, a fast edge out. Each stretch is a current loop of its own; the emission adds them per harmonic, each with its own spectrum, so the fast edge after the buffer counts even where the slow input has none [A76].

A part is a logic buffer when its symbol has the GT labels buf_in / buf_out (a pair per channel, or one list each by position: buf_in:[1,3], buf_out:[6,4]; a quad comparator's four units the same way), or a Threshold Rising, Threshold Falling or Hysteresis field and pins whose names pair input and output (1A with 1Y, A with Y, IN with OUT); or set its model to buffer on the Parts tab (for a dual: its pins as input, output pairs, e.g. 1, 6, 3, 4). Its values come from its symbol's fields, which an HTTP library can fill in from a parts database (InvenTree parameters of the same names), at the supply it runs at:

  • Rise Time: its output's edge (default 2.5 ns);
  • Zout: its output resistance (default 25 Ω), Cin: its input capacitance (default 5 pF) [A73];
  • Threshold Rising and Threshold Falling (VT+, VT−), or Hysteresis (their difference): the noise limit at its input.

Detection crosses a resistor up to 100 kΩ in series into a buffer's input (the RC filter's 10 kΩ, above the series limit of 1 kΩ) and lists it under Pass through [A74]; a pull-up to a supply stays out. The Bode of such a path ends at the first buffer's input: the buffer's output has no linear response to the source. With a hysteresis, the buffer's input is a victim of the bridges and power stages by itself, its noise checked against the hysteresis [A75]. The path's description shows it as U25 (buffer).

Add GBW, Aol, Zout (and Cin, PM) fields to the symbol for its real model; without them the defaults above apply, with a note. A settings file made before the part was recognised keeps open until the parts are refreshed (Reload board, or groundtruth init), which then makes it an amplifier.

The transfer function is shown two ways: in the part names, for the ideal circuit (ideal op-amps, the parts at their design values without parasitics, an ideal source, no load), e.g. a Sallen-Key's H(s) = 1 / (C1·C2·R1·R2·s² + C2·(R1 + R2)·s + 1), over the Bode plot and in the report (up to 15 parts); and numerically, for the full circuit, in the engineering normal form K · Π(1 + s/ω) · Π(1 + s/(Q·ω) + s²/ω²) / [ … ] with every pole's and zero's frequency and Q (K the DC gain), in the summary line and the report. The poles and zeros are exact (the circuit's eigenvalues, each track as its lumped π section; a note says up to which frequency that is exact); a pole and a zero closer than 0.1 % of the pole's distance from the jω axis are left out of the lists (they change |H| by less than that). Each op-amp's loop gain is computed by breaking its loop at its output: its crossover frequency and phase margin (amber below 45°: the circuit rings; red below 20°: close to oscillating). Simulate's emission estimate takes the path's currents from the same circuit.

How the transfer function is calculated. No circuit simulator (SPICE or similar) is used: GroundTruth builds the circuit's equations itself and solves them with two libraries, SymPy for the algebra and NumPy / SciPy for the numbers.

  1. The circuit from the board. From the path's nets outwards, every part with a model, every op-amp channel and every track and via becomes an element between named nodes (the nets; ground and the supply rails are AC ground). This is the same circuit for both forms below; the ideal form simply leaves out what a designer's equation leaves out. The path's own nets (from the driver forward and from the receiver backward, through op-amps only from input to output) are never AC ground, whatever their name or pin types: a battery divider's VBAT_DIV is a signal there, and a note says so. Its own nets and its op-amps' feedback are always in the circuit; beyond them it holds up to 60 nets (a note names what it leaves out). Parts not fitted (DNP) are left out; a part on its nets without a model, set to open, or a resistor model on more than two nets (an array: give it its pins) is left out and named in a note, as is an op-amp left with no DC path from its output to IN− (its DC gain is then the open-loop one). When the path's driver pin is an op-amp's output, the source stands for that op-amp. The load resistance is at every receiver. A resistor or capacitor array (RN1 on more than two nets) is taken as isolated elements, pad 1 to the last pad, 2 to the one before and so on, with a note; a bussed array needs a model per element. An analog switch that is on is modelled as a short (or resistor) with pins [a, b] on its two switched pins. A circuit of more than 2000 unknowns gets its poles and zeros fitted to the sweep instead of solved exactly (a note says so). The H(s) in part names is the circuit as designed: a note names the driver's resistance and the load it leaves out.
  2. The equations: modified nodal analysis. Kirchhoff's current law at every node, written as one matrix equation (G + s·C)·x = b·u: x the node voltages plus a current for each voltage source, op-amp and inductor; G the conductances and the connections, C the capacitances (and the inductors), u the input. This is the standard method of circuit simulators (Ho, Ruehli and Brennan, 1975).
  3. The ideal H(s), in part names (SymPy). Each resistor is the symbol R1, each capacitor 1/(s·C2), each inductor s·L1, at their design values without parasitics; the input an ideal voltage source, no load. Each op-amp is ideal: a nullor (Franco, 2015). Its two inputs are forced to the same voltage and its output supplies whatever current that takes, so its gain, bandwidth and output impedance drop out and only the feedback network is left. SymPy solves the matrix equation symbolically (its exact LU solve on the symbol matrix; the determinant with the division-free Berkowitz method decides whether the circuit has a unique answer). The result v(out)/v(in) is cancelled to one fraction N(s)/D(s) and written with D's constant term 1, so N's constant term is the DC gain: e.g. −R4/R1 / (C2·C5·R3·R4·s² + C5·(R1·R3 + R1·R4 + R3·R4)/R1·s + 1). Up to 15 parts: beyond that a symbolic solve grows too large to read.
  4. The full circuit, in numbers (NumPy / SciPy). The same equations with every value filled in: each capacitor with its ESR, ESL and capacitance at its DC voltage, each track as a transmission line (or a lumped π section for the poles), the source's and the load's impedance, and each op-amp as its datasheet macromodel (Aol with its pole at GBW/Aol, a second pole, Zout, the input capacitances: above). The Bode plot solves the matrix equation at each frequency. The poles are the generalised eigenvalues of the matrix pencil (G, −C) (SciPy's linalg.eig, the QZ algorithm: Golub and Van Loan); the zeros the eigenvalues of the system matrix bordered with the input and the output (Rosenbrock, 1970). The matrices are scaled first, so parts from pF to MΩ stay well conditioned. From the poles and zeros comes the normal form K · Π(1 + s/ω) · Π(1 + s/(Q·ω) + s²/ω²) / […]; from the same equations the loop gain and the step response.

The ideal form is what you design with; the numeric one is what the board does, so they differ where the board's details matter: a real op-amp's limited bandwidth, a source impedance (25 Ω by default) in series with the input resistor, a capacitor's DC-bias capacitance. Both are checked against independent results: textbook closed forms of inverting, non-inverting, Sallen-Key, multiple-feedback and integrator circuits; an independent solve of the same circuits with Lcapy (validation audit round 7); and TI's OPA376 multiple-feedback low-pass, drawn in KiCad in examples/opamp_mfb_lowpass. For that board the ideal H(s) read from the copper is exactly TI's Eq. 1: DC gain −R4/R1 = −4.98, f₀ = 1/(2π·√(R3·R4·C2·C5)) = 21.0 kHz, 3 dB of Chebyshev peaking.

The step response: the path's (and each op-amp output's) response to a 1 V step at the driver, from rest, on the plot under the Bode plot: the final value (the DC gain) dashed with its ±2 % band, the settling time dotted; a filter's milliseconds and an op-amp's nanoseconds each on a panel of their own. The step tile gives the overshoot (amber from 20 %), the 10-90 % rise time and the settling time into ±2 %; an undershoot (the first move the wrong way: a right-half-plane zero) is named in the summary line and the report. It is computed in closed form from the same circuit's poles and zeros (partial fractions), so it is exact for the circuit, not a fit, and so are its rise, overshoot and settling times, however long a lightly damped pole rings; shown over 12 time constants of the slowest pole that shows in it. Where that form is numerically unsafe (poles too close together) the circuit is integrated in the time domain instead (the second-order backward differentiation formula, as SPICE does for stiff circuits). An AC-coupled path (a zero at the origin: a high-pass, a coupling capacitor) has a final value of 0: its line gives the peak, when it comes and when it has decayed into ±2 % of the peak. A path with an integrator (a pole at the origin) has no final value and no step response.

Limits. A path's Max bandwidth (Signal paths tab; max_bandwidth, e.g. 1 kHz for an ADC input's anti-alias filter) is checked at its receiver: its bandwidth, where it has fallen 3 dB below its passband peak, above the limit is a bandwidth_limit issue (also when it does not fall 3 dB within the sweep while the sweep reaches above the limit). On an op-amp solved in its circuit (no Gain field), its Bandwidth field is its stage's limit: the stage's own transfer, from the previous op-amp's output (or the driver pin) to its output, so an upstream filter does not count against it; a filter after the op-amp is not part of its stage (use the path's Max bandwidth for it). A loop with less than 45° of phase margin is an opamp_stability warning, below 20° an error; give the op-amp PM (its datasheet's phase margin at unity gain) or Fp2: with one pole the margin is always near 90°. With the Bode button the findings are notes and a tile; Simulate makes them issues.

Labelling a signal path

Detect finds most clock and data paths on its own, from net names and pin types. Label a path when it:

  • looks like a supply (a name with RAIL, VIN or +3V3 in it, or a power pin on the net), for example an ADC input that measures a rail;
  • gets the wrong driver (both ends are bidirectional pins, or the schematic has no pin types);
  • runs through an op-amp. Detect only crosses an op-amp that is labelled, or set to amplifier on the Parts tab;
  • runs through a resistor above 1 kΩ or a capacitor, which a path doesn't cross unless it is labelled or named in Pass through.

What to label. Label only the parts at the ends of the path and its op-amps:

PartGT fieldMeaning
the part that drives the pathsig_drv:3pin 3 drives the signal
each op-amp on the wayamp_in:1, amp_out:4the stage's input pin (non-inverting input for a follower) and its output pin; the part becomes an amplifier in the Parts tab
the part that receives itsig_rx:9pin 9 receives it

Leave the resistors, ferrites and filter capacitors in between unlabelled: the path finds them itself. sig:9 (no direction) only says the net on pin 9 is a signal; Detect then guesses its driver. sig on its own, on a 2-pin part, marks both its nets as signals.

Pins are pad numbers (sig_rx:9) or pin names (sig_rx:PA2), as in the schematic symbol. For a dual or quad op-amp, name the pins of the stage on the path (amp_in:5, amp_out:7 for the second unit). Several labels on one part go in the same field, separated by commas: a microcontroller with two ADC inputs on two paths gets sig_rx:9, sig_rx:10.

Several channels of one op-amp (a dual or a quad whose units are on different paths): write one group per channel, amp_in, amp_inv, amp_out, one group after the other in the same GT field. A new channel starts where a role comes again; the channels are named after the part and their order, as the schematic names its units. A quad op-amp U3 with three followers:

U3A+ pin 2, − pin 1, output pin 14
U3B+ pin 4, − pin 5, output pin 6
U3C+ pin 9, − pin 8, output pin 7

gets, on every unit of the symbol (the board's footprint carries one GT field for the whole part):

GT = amp_in:2, amp_inv:1, amp_out:14, amp_in:4, amp_inv:5, amp_out:6, amp_in:9, amp_inv:8, amp_out:7

Each channel is then a stage of its own: a path through U3A's input runs to U3A's output, never into U3B's. amp_inv is needed here because the part has four pins named −, so the name can't tell which one is the channel's. The labels line flags a group without its amp_in or amp_out (that channel is left out).

The compact form: the same channels as lists, one per role, matched by position (the first entries are U3A, the second U3B, and so on):

GT = amp_in:[2,4,9], amp_inv:[1,5,8], amp_out:[14,6,7]

is read exactly as the groups above. Every list has one entry per channel; write - for a channel without amp_inv (amp_inv:[1,-,8]). Lists of different lengths, or without their amp_in or amp_out list, are not read, and a - in an amp_in or amp_out list leaves that channel out; the labels line says which. A quad comparator or a dual Schmitt trigger (logic buffers) takes the same form, e.g. a quad comparator's four units: buf_in:[11,4,6,8], buf_out:[13,16,15,14]. A field may also hold both forms: the lists' channels come after those written as groups.

How Detect turns the labels into a path. It starts at a labelled net and follows 2-pin parts and labelled op-amps (input net to output net) to the next labelled net. It never crosses into a ground, a reference net or an unlabelled supply. Branches that end at an unlabelled net (a load resistor to ground, a filter capacitor) are dropped from the path. They still count in Simulate, as parts across the path. Parts on the path that don't pass a signal by default (a resistor over 1 kΩ) go into its Pass through list. The path is named after one of its nets with a real name (not Net-(…)). One row then covers every net of the path. Op-amps get their gain from a Gain field on the symbol or from the Parts tab (1 when neither is given). Don't put a sig label on an op-amp in the path: it would end the path there. Use amp_in / amp_out instead.

Example (a current-sense output read by an ADC through a buffer and a resistor):

U42 (current-sense amplifier)sig_drv:3
U41 (op-amp follower)amp_in:1, amp_out:4
R41 4.7 kΩ, C41 100 nF to groundno label
U53 (microcontroller)sig_rx:9

Detect gives one path, U42.3 → U41 ×1 → R41 4k7 → U53.9, named after the ADC net, with R41 in Pass through and C41 as a part across the path.

Steps:

  1. In the schematic editor, add a field named GT to each symbol, either in Symbol Properties or, for several at once, in the Symbol Fields Table (add a GT column).
  2. Update PCB from Schematic (F8) and save the board. The labels reach the board as footprint fields and change no net names.
  3. In GroundTruth, press Reload board, then Detect…. The new path is ticked. An unreviewed row that covered part of it before is offered as REPLACES: keep it ticked and press OK. Reviewed and hand-made rows are never changed; edit or remove them yourself.
  4. Set the path's frequency and rise time on the Signal paths tab, then mark it reviewed.

Shortcut: select one bit of copper on each net of the path in the PCB editor and press Add KiCad selection (see A whole path from KiCad). GroundTruth works out the same labels and adds the path at once.

Re-annotated board: the labels move with the parts, so when the parts get new references (U46 becomes U45) a converter named after its label (buck conv3 for conv3_*) whose parts no longer fit takes the parts its labels now name at the next Run checks or Simulate; the status line says what changed (one Undo step). The command line does the same for its run.

Note: writing the GT labels into the schematic automatically needs KiCad 11. KiCad 10's API can't change a schematic, so on KiCad 10 GroundTruth shows the labels in a message for you to add by hand (step 1; Ctrl+C copies the message). The path is in the settings either way, and Simulate uses it right away; the labels only keep it in the design, so that Detect finds it again later.

The line at the bottom of the Converters tab (Labels (GT field)) names the parts that carry signal and op-amp labels, and lists as problems a GT word that is not a label (a typing error), a label naming a pin its part doesn't have, an op-amp with amp_in but no amp_out (or the other way round; per channel of a dual or quad), and label lists that can't be read (amp_in:[2,4,9] with an amp_out list of another length, or none). If a labelled path still doesn't appear in Detect…, check that every net of the path is joined to the next by a 2-pin part or a labelled op-amp.

Configurations, Back to last settings, Undo

The settings are what the tabs show; every change is saved at once, and the window opens with them as they were left (the last configuration). To keep a set-up and come back to it (these paths, this bridge's operating point, these ticks, these run settings), save it as a configuration: a file of its own that holds what is simulated and how. Loading it later puts exactly that back on the tabs, so the same simulation runs again.

In a configurationthe rows of Signal paths, Converters, Bridges, DC loads, Power integrity and Signal integrity (pairs, targets, limits), each with its tick; the what-if edits; the run settings (analysis: emission limits, distance, frequencies, solver; thermal; victims).
Shared by allthe part models (Parts tab, from the schematic's fields), through-hole pin values, reference nets, the board stack, safety voltages, the check rules and severities, waived issues, drawing and report options: facts about the board that do not change between simulations.

The top bar's Configuration ▾:

  • Save configuration as…: a name (and a note) and a Folder (Browse… picks another one); written to <folder>\<name>.gtconfig.yaml, by default groundtruth\configs\ beside the settings (or the folder of the configuration in use), and then the configuration in use: the window's title shows its name, with a * once the tabs differ from the file.
  • Save configuration: the one in use, written again with the tabs as they are.
  • Load configuration…: a configuration file (any folder). Its sections replace the tabs' (one it lacks is emptied: a configuration says exactly what is simulated); the part models stay as they are now. Parts whose models changed since the configuration was saved, or that are gone, are named after loading, and so are nets or parts the board does not have.
  • Load from a past run ▸: the configuration of one of the newest kept runs (the History tab has the same as Load its configuration). The run's part models are not put back: for that, its whole settings file is in the run's folder.
  • Back to last settings: the settings as they were before the last load. Each load keeps the settings it replaces (configs\.before-load.gtconfig.yaml), so choosing it again goes back to the configuration left: it toggles.
  • Save settings now: writes the settings file at once (it is written after every change anyway).

Undo and Redo (Ctrl+Z, Ctrl+Y or Ctrl+Shift+Z) step through every change of the settings in this window: an edit or a tick on any tab, a tab's Detect…, a waived issue, a loaded configuration (one step), up to 50 steps. Their tooltips name the step. In a text field, Ctrl+Z undoes the typing in that field instead. Greyed while a job runs.

On the command line, groundtruth check | simulate | bode BOARD --config FILE runs a saved configuration in place of the settings file's (for that run only; the file is not changed), e.g. in CI. groundtruth verify BOARD --config FILE --thresholds T.gtthresholds.yaml is the release gate built on it: the configuration simulated and judged against pass / fail criteria (margins, loop inductance, efficiency, issue counts, temperature), one JUnit test per criterion for GitLab, exit 0 pass, 1 fail, 2 when the common settings file or one of the two files is missing or invalid (docs/guide/ci.md in the repository).

The settings file

GroundTruth keeps everything for a board in one folder next to it, groundtruth, so the KiCad project folder stays tidy: the settings file, the simulation history and (offered first) the exported reports. Everything you enter is kept in groundtruth\<board>.groundtruth.yaml: the nets, converters, bridges, part models, rules (thresholds, severities), analysis settings, waivers and what-if edits. Commit it with the board. It can be edited by hand; the plugin reads it when the window opens. The same file drives the groundtruth command line for CI, with the same results.

Useful analysis settings: max_nodes (the grid budget of each model: lower it for speed or memory), threads (solves in parallel), conducted (cispr22, cispr25, cispr32, mil461 or none) and conducted_class, limit_class (CISPR 32 class A or B for radiated), radiated_limit (another radiated limit: MIL-STD-461G RE102 by name, e.g. re102_ground_army or re102_aircraft_external (fixed wing, from 2 MHz) or re102_helicopter (from 10 kHz), at 1 m with the standard's bandwidths; or a curve of your own: a name, the distance and its points), mil461 (the MIL-STD-461 revision, G or H: under H the ships' CE101 limit runs to 20 kHz; nothing else here differs), ce101 (MIL-STD-461 CE101, the input leads' current from 30 Hz to 10 kHz, ships under 461H to 20 kHz: aircraft or ships; a motor bridge's commutation lines, strongest at six times the electrical frequency, read through the bus capacitors and the test leads, which can resonate in this band; shown as CE101 margin and in the emission plots), gpu (auto, on, off). Further: max_solves (10: the solves a model's refinement may add), distance_m (auto: the standard's, 10 m below 1 GHz and 3 m above for CISPR 32), ground_reflection (true; false leaves the floor's reflection out everywhere), driver_ohms (25) and load_pf (5) for paths without their own source and load, plane_capacitance, planes (auto or nearest), cavity_max_nodes (40 000: the board-wide model's grid budget; max_nodes is 100 000), via_plating_um (25: a via's plating, for its resistance, heat, current density and IPC-2221 capacity), ce101_source_v and ce101_load_a. In the plugin, Preferences sets the resolution and can replace threads, gpu, sparse, plane_screening, thermal.quality, the electro-thermal settings, thermal.capacity_c, safety.solder_mask and safety.esd_mm on this computer; those choices are not written to the file.

The checks' thresholds are under rules:: stitch_distance_mm 2 (a return via this close to a layer change), decap_distance_mm 5, edge_factor 3 (a trace closer to its plane's edge than this many heights above it), min_gap_mm 0.1, detour_warning 1.25 and detour_error 2 (the loop's inductance over the ideal loop's), emi_margin_db 6 (radiated and conducted: a warning within it, an error over the limit), resonance_db 10 (plane_resonance), overshoot_warning 25 % and overshoot_error 50 %, current_density_warning 50 and current_density_error 100 A/mm² (copper over 1 mm, a via's plating), via_load_warning 100 and via_load_error 150 % of a via's IPC-2221 capacity. rules: severity: {check: error | warning | info | off} changes a check's severity or switches it off (the check names are in the Issues tab's list).

Speed, memory and the GPU

  • A board with a few nets simulates in a minute or two; a power stage takes 30–70 s; a motor bridge with its victims and a sweep several minutes.
  • Large models need memory: each bridge model on an 8-layer board can take a few GB while it is factorised. When the models of all nets and stages would not fit in the free memory at once, Simulate solves them in groups, one after the other (the board-wide model last); the results are the same, it may take a little longer. Simulate does not stop when memory runs short: before each large factorisation it checks what it will need against what is free (on a compute server, within the job's limit) and frees the factorisations it has kept, waits for the others being made, or makes a leaner one (the least fill: more iterations, the same results). A solve that runs out anyway is repeated on its own with everything freed, then with the leaner factorisation; only if even that fails is that one frequency left unsolved (a note, and a band_unsolved warning naming what it misses), and the rest of the run finishes. The run's notes say what it had to do. To avoid it: close other programs, choose fewer Solver threads or a lower Resolution (above 1×) in Preferences, or lower analysis.max_nodes.
  • Two optional speed-ups, each installed once per computer with an installer in the plugin's tools folder (Plugin and Content Manager's install folder: Documents\KiCad\10.0\3rdparty\plugins\io_github_afkiwers_groundtruth\tools on Windows). Plane screening: a plane between two others

The field solver models each plane layer as one sheet carrying one current. For a plane with other planes on both sides (a power plane between two grounds, an inner ground between a signal layer and a power layer) that is too little at high frequency: the copper has a different field on each face, and one sheet cannot screen one side from the other. Against the exact result for such a stack, one sheet per layer reads 6 % low (0.2 mm dielectric, 35 µm copper) to 26 % low (0.1 mm, 70 µm) at 30 MHz and above, and a floating plane between a pair (no net) does not screen at all (+5 %). At low frequency the single sheet is exact.

Plane screening: on splits such a layer into two sheets, one per face, joined through the copper thickness by the exact one-dimensional model of a metal slab (a sum and a difference mode). On the test stacks it is within 0.1-0.7 % of the exact result from 10 kHz to 100 MHz at 0.4, 0.2 and 0.1 mm cells. Only a layer with other layers' copper above and below it over at least 16 of its grid nodes is split; a two-layer board, a plane with copper on one side only, and the DC models (where both faces carry one current) are unchanged.

What it costs. A split layer is one more sheet (more unknowns, more memory for the inductance kernels), and each solve takes a little longer. On a six-layer board (four layers split) a clock net's Simulate took 129 s with it off and 155 s on (933 GMRES iterations off, 870 on) on the CPU, and a whole Simulate of that board's 6 nets and 4 converters 800 s off and 1 256 s on (8 796 and 8 814 iterations); the kernels of a board with many split layers take up to 2.8 times the memory. Your board's figure depends on how many layers split and on how much of the Simulate is board-wide solving. Emission and loop figures also move: on that board the loop inductance changed by 1 % and an emission margin by up to a few dB (part of that from where the solver's adaptive frequencies land).

When to turn it on: a stack with a plane between two others where you care about the high-frequency loop or emission (above a few tens of MHz): stripline-like inner layers, ground / power / ground. Leave it off for quick runs, boards with two layers or with signals over one plane, and when the answer only has to rank layouts.

The two ways to set it (a run takes the command line's value, else this computer's Preferences, else the settings file's):

  • In the settings file (travels with the board, so a CI run reproduces it): analysis: plane_screening: true (false, the default, when not set). On the command line: groundtruth simulate BOARD --plane-screening on (or off) for that run alone.
  • In Preferences (this computer only): Plane screening, From the settings file (the default), off or on. The (i) button beside it opens this section.

Source and assumptions: Dowell 1966, Ferreira 1994 (the slab's two-port); REFERENCES A92 (the split and its impedance) and A93 (which layers split).

Compute server: set up and run (Server jobs tab)

Simulate, Suggest fixes, Check in Simulate, Compare what-if, Thermal and Current capacity can run on a powerful PC on your local network: you keep working in KiCad on your own computer, the solving happens on the server, and the results show here as usual. Several people can share one server; their jobs wait in a queue. KiCad does not need to be running on the server.

Update the servers from here: Preferences, Compute server, Servers lists the server and the others of its cluster (and any other server with this key the network answers from), each with its version against this plugin's (the out-of-date ones in orange: an earlier build, also one that still takes this plugin's jobs because only the server's own program changed; a server is asked at whichever of its addresses answers from this computer). Update (the selected server) or Update all installs this plugin's own version on them (after a confirmation listing each server's version now and after); each row shows how far its update is. A development copy of the plugin carries no server package: its buttons stay off.

Only a server installed as a service takes this, and only for the options of its own fixed list: the plugin sends an option's name, never a package or an address, and the request is signed with the server's key like every other. How the simulation works (the algorithms)

Simulate uses one field solver for everything electrical. Nets, converters, DC paths, bridges and the fix search differ only in the current they drive into it. Thermal has a solver of its own. Where each formula and method comes from, and what the model assumes, is in References and assumptions.

This is not FEM. The field solver uses PEEC (partial element equivalent circuit), an integral-equation method from the method-of-moments family. Only the copper is meshed: each layer's copper is cut into small strips, and vias, pins and parts become branches between layers. Each strip gets its resistance, its partial inductance to every other strip (from the free-space field), and the capacitance between facing planes. The board becomes one large circuit, solved at each frequency. The thermal and DC solves come from the PCB Thermal solver: a finite-volume grid, not FEM either.

PEEC and FEM compared. FEM (the finite element method) meshes the whole volume: copper, dielectric and the air around the board, out to an artificial boundary. Its system is sparse but very large. PEEC meshes only the conductors and puts the coupling through space into its kernels, so there is no air mesh. Its system is smaller but dense, which a regular grid lets it apply with FFTs.

  • Why PEEC here. A PCB is thin copper (35 µm) spread over a large area. FEM would need millions of flat elements and a robust mesher for any KiCad geometry. PEEC gives the copper's currents directly, and those are the questions GroundTruth answers: return paths, loop inductance, hot loops, plane resonances and DC paths. It is also fast enough to solve dozens of frequencies per net and to try every candidate fix. Full-wave FEM is slow at the low frequencies where power stages live (DC to a few MHz). Commercial power- and signal-integrity tools use PEEC, method-of-moments or 2.5D solvers for the same reasons.
  • Where FEM (or another full 3D method such as FDTD) would shine. Radiation with the enclosure and the cables in the model: GroundTruth estimates radiated emission from the board's current loops alone. Local 3D detail: current crowding inside a big connector pin or a dense via field, or skin and proximity effects in thick copper. Independent reference solutions, to check where PEEC's quasi-static, planar assumptions stop holding at high frequency (see What the numbers mean, and their limits).

The field solver: quasi-static PEEC (partial element equivalent circuit, in the frequency domain)

  • The grid. Every plane layer is cut into the same square grid. Copper is sampled on a finer sub-grid (≤ 0.15 mm), so thin webs between via antipads stay connected and a slot still cuts. Tracks narrower than a cell are followed along their centre line and kept joined; where another net's pour takes a cell, the track crosses it as a lumped branch with the track's own resistance and inductance. Nets are never joined, even when their clearance is smaller than a cell, and neither are two pieces of one net's copper that do not touch on the layer. Each strip conducts with exactly the width of copper that crosses its cell border (a track at an angle crosses it w / cos θ long, which keeps its resistance), and each node's current density is taken over its cell's own copper, so that the heat it gives Thermal is exactly the strips' I²·R.
  • The unknowns:
    • the currents in the x and y strips between cells on every layer;
    • the currents through the plane-pair capacitances, the vias, the plated pads and the parts;
    • the voltage of every node.
    The equations are Kirchhoff's voltage law over each strip and branch, Z·I − A·V = −jω·ψ (ψ: the field of the known signal or switch current), and Kirchhoff's current law at each node.
  • The partial elements:
    • a strip has R(f), including skin effect (the exact result for a copper slab with the field on one face, as over its return plane) and the copper thickness from the stackup, plus jωL;
    • every strip couples magnetically to every other strip on every layer (mutual partial inductance);
    • two layers with copper and none in between form a parallel-plate capacitance ε₀·εᵣ·area/t, scaled by the cell's copper fraction and with dielectric loss (tan δ), the dielectrics between them in series;
    • a via is its barrel resistance plus partial self-inductance; a through-hole pin (a connector's) is a solid conductor joined to every layer all around its barrel, so its current enters the copper around the whole ring, not at one point;
    • a part is its model's Z(f) plus 0.3 nH mounting inductance between its two sides' metal (a terminal, a FET's drain or source), each soldered over the whole of its pads (75 µm of solder, a few tenths of a mΩ on a large pad): the current hands over to the part where the copper brings it to the pad, mostly at the edge it arrives at, falling off across the pad, not at a few points of it. That metal is taken as a perfect conductor unless the part says otherwise, so a part whose terminal isn't given shows its pad dark with a sharp bright rim: the cue to give it. An inductor's clip or a capacitor's or resistor's end cap is thin, about as resistive as the board copper under it (0.17-0.77 mΩ per square), and then shares the pad's current with the copper across the whole pad. Give it as a part field (an HTTP lib parameter, or typed into the symbol): Terminal Sheet Resistance in Ω per square (0.77mΩ, or a bare number in Ω), or Terminal Metal as thickness and material (0.15mm bronze: copper, brass, bronze, becu, tellurium); the sheet resistance wins when both are there, 0 is a perfect conductor. A transistor modelled as a resistor (a surge stopper, a reverse-polarity FET) with a drain tab has its on-resistance spread evenly over the tab instead, as its die's cells are: the current leaves the copper all across the tab, not only at its edge and its vias (the pin value entry:tab keeps one soldered metal). A power stage's switch with a tab (a pad at least 1 mm wide on a side: a silicon FET's drain, a diode's cathode) is soldered over its pads the same way, so the stage's currents enter the copper where it brings them; a chip-scale GaN FET's narrow bars keep their current spread evenly over each pin's copper, which its die does (the pin value entry overrides). Both tabs read the Terminal Sheet Resistance too: a FET's drain tab is then that sheet under its die, and a switch's tab (a diode's cathode, an IC's exposed pad in a stage) takes the stage's current in evenly over the sheet; without it they are perfect plates, dark with a sharp rim like any other part. The part's element covers its whole terminal unless the pin value metal (a GT field's D=4x3.5, see Detection labels) gives it a smaller rectangle: a FET's die then stands only on that part of its tab's sheet, and a terminal's current enters its sheet only there.
    The integrals use Gauss–Legendre quadrature near by, filaments far away, and numerical integration for the self term.
  • Fast products. The mutual inductance between two strips depends only on their offset, so each layer pair is a convolution. It is applied with FFTs (double precision; single precision stalled the solver near resonances) and a compiled multiply-add.
  • The linear solve: GMRES (right-preconditioned, restarted):
    • Gram–Schmidt orthogonalisation, repeated only where cancellation happens;
    • the basis vectors are stored in single precision and every product with them is done in double precision (mixed-precision GMRES): half the memory and half the memory traffic, the same iterations at the solver's tolerance; the answer is checked on the true residual in double precision;
    • Givens rotations keep the small least-squares problem triangular;
    • it stops as soon as the true residual meets the tolerance (checked when the running estimate first says so);
    • the restart length follows a memory budget;
    • a solve that stops converging is marked unsolved, and its band is named in the results, never silently filled in.
  • The preconditioner is an exact sparse factorisation (SuperLU, or Apple Accelerate's or Intel MKL PARDISO's complex symmetric LDLᵀ where available: Sparse solver in Preferences) of the system's Schur complement Aᵀ·D⁻¹·A. D has two forms:
    • each strip's own impedance, for the route models, which are inductive;
    • per grid position, a block that couples all layers, for the board-wide model. It keeps the planes' cavity modes; without it the solver needs thousands of iterations above about 600 MHz.
  • Reuse.
    • One factorisation serves every net at its frequency. Nearby frequencies (within ±25 %) borrow it within an iteration budget; past that budget the solver factorises again.
    • The plan of which frequency borrows what is fixed before solving, so the same board gives bit-identical results whatever the thread count.
    • Candidate vias and capacitors (Suggest fixes) and a converter's LISN are added as low-rank (Woodbury) updates of the existing factorisation, and their solves start from the baseline solution.
    • With a GPU, the FFTs, products and orthogonalisation run there; the factorisations stay on the CPU (see Speed, memory and the GPU).

Signal nets

  • Two models per net, because one grid can't be both fine enough for the loop next to the trace and large enough for the whole board:
    • a fine model around the route, with R + jωL;
    • a coarser model of the whole board that adds the plane capacitance, for displacement currents and plane resonances.
    Their loop moments combine as m(f) = mroute(f0) + [mboard(f) − mboard(f0)], so the coarse grid's bias at f0 cancels.
  • The current on the trace. The driver is a Thévenin source (resistance, inductance, capacitance) with the trapezoid's harmonics. The route is a chain of lossless transmission lines (Z0 and εeff of each stretch from the board's own cross-section there, solved as the Signal integrity checks solve it: the copper around the track, the stackup's dielectrics and the solder mask; closed forms where no clean cut exists) and series parts, solved at each harmonic and loaded by the receivers. A net's current_a replaces this: the line's peak current at the fundamental. Shunt parts and op-amp stages become current loops of their own, added per harmonic with their own currents.
  • Frequencies. The solves happen at actual harmonics of the signal. The solver then adds solves in the gaps where a resonance could hide or where the estimate is closest to the limit, and always solves the worst-margin harmonic itself. Between solves, |m| is interpolated on log-log axes. Which frequencies get a solve is chosen with the floor's reflection counted at every frequency, also above 1 GHz where the reported field leaves it out: the solves are then the same whatever the test site, and a resonance above 1 GHz is not skipped for the 6 dB.
  • Emission. A small loop radiates |E| = 1.316·10⁻¹⁴·f²·|m|·I/r, times √(1 + 1/(k·r)²) for the near field (k = 2πf/c: it counts at 1 m and at low frequency), doubled for the ground reflection (CISPR 32: up to 1 GHz, where the test site's floor reflects; above it the standard measures over absorber, so the free-space field is taken; a radiated limit of your own, RE102 or a curve, keeps the reflection at every frequency, and analysis.ground_reflection: false leaves it out everywhere). A signal path's current I is taken with its phase along the route (the largest value of the route's radiation integral over direction): the mean current while the route is short against the wavelength, more where a standing wave would cancel in the mean but still radiates towards the trace's ends. Harmonics closer together than the receiver's bandwidth add up in one reading; a path through a logic buffer, evaluated at a thinned choice of its harmonics, reads what all of them within the bandwidth add up to. The receiver's bandwidth adds harmonics that fall in it in phase. The result is compared with CISPR 32.

Power stages and bridges

  • The hot loop. The switches are current filaments carrying the commutated current as a trapezoid pulse train; their pours are solved copper.
    • The pulse's top carries the inductor's ripple (from the inductor's L): it rises from I − ΔI/2 to I + ΔI/2 through a buck's high side and falls through a boost's rectifier. That fills the nulls a flat pulse has (the even harmonics near 50 % duty, what interleaved phases leave at the input). The L·di/dt overshoot is taken at the peak, I + ΔI/2. It was checked against the Fourier transform of the sampled waveform.
    • The loop inductance comes from a series-LC fit at 30 and 100 MHz.
    • The ringing is a second-order resonance with C_oss, and an edge-charge factor adds the C_oss·dv/dt current. It was checked against a time-domain simulation of the switching cell.
  • Conducted emission. The LISN is added to the loop as a low-rank update. Its current, per ampere of loop current, is interpolated over frequency and summed over the receiver bandwidth. For spread spectrum, the receiver itself is simulated: each harmonic's sweep passes a Gaussian filter swept across the band, then the standard's peak or average detector. Common mode is C·dV/dt of the switch node through the two LISNs.
  • DC paths are the same solver at 10 Hz, where the copper is purely resistive. All stages share one model of the whole board, so their currents add (superposition) in the "currents together" view. Via currents are checked against IPC-2221.
  • Fix search. Candidates are ranked one by one. They are then picked greedily: in each round the remaining candidates are re-evaluated with the chosen ones in place, and the best is added.

Thermal (the PCB Thermal solver)

  • Steady-state 3-D heat conduction on a block-structured mesh, refined where the heat is. Convection and radiation leave through the board's faces (correlations for h).
  • The heat comes from two sources:
    • the copper's Joule heat ρ·|J|²·t from the DC current maps;
    • the parts' losses: conduction, the switching edges, Q_oss·V·f, the dead times' reverse conduction and Q_rr, a diode's V_f, the inductor's DCR and core loss, connector pins.
  • The system is solved with conjugate gradients, preconditioned by algebraic multigrid (pyamg; a direct solve for small models). The copper's resistivity follows each node's temperature, by iteration.

Each model is checked against an analytic result in the test suite. Examples: a microstrip's return-current spread and loop inductance against Wheeler, a pour over its plane against Hammerstad, parallel-plate capacitance and the cavity's first resonance, current conservation across every cut between planes, and a strip's I²R heating.

What the numbers mean, and their limits

  • Emission figures are for ranking and comparing layouts, not a compliance prediction. The model is quasi-static (no radiation from plane edges), it estimates the differential-mode loop of each net or hot loop, and common-mode currents on cables, which often dominate real emissions, are not modelled (a converter's common-mode budget is the exception).
  • The board-wide model is quasi-static: above the frequency at which the board is a quarter wavelength across in its laminate (about 350 MHz for 100 mm of FR-4) its emissions are indicative upper bounds. The spectrum shades that range grey, the issue says so, and a finer Resolution than 4× buys no accuracy there. analysis.max_frequency in the settings file (e.g. 1GHz; auto = the edge's own band, a decade above 1/(π·rise time)) cuts the band evaluated: faster runs, or a test plan's own top; the range above it is hatched and the notes say it was not computed, not that it passed.
  • Plane resonance peaks are upper estimates (the model has no radiation loss); margins set by a resonance are good to a few dB.
  • The edge time sets the high-frequency content and L·di/dt: use the fastest edge at the operating current (a GaN bridge at 100 A switches in about a nanosecond, at 1 A on a bench in 100 ns).
  • Thermal: steady state; heat leaves through the board's faces only (no heatsinks, enclosure or part bodies yet), so large parts come out on the hot side.
  • Results depend on what you enter: frequencies, edge times, currents, part models. Orange rows are still guesses.
  • Each assumption the model makes is listed, with its reason, under References and assumptions.
  • Not validated on real hardware. The solver is checked against analytic results (loop inductance within 1–4 % of Wheeler's formula, the return current against image theory, plate capacitance exactly), but the board-level figures have not all been compared with measurements.

How the copper and the substrate count. All from the board's stackup (Board Setup › Physical Stackup):

  • Copper thickness: each track's and plane's resistance, at high frequency only the skin depth's share of it (resistance rises above a few MHz); a track's self-inductance (a thicker strip slightly less); the cross-sections of Signal integrity. Plane cells' mutual inductances treat the copper as flat sheets.
  • Dielectric thickness: the layers' heights, so how far a track is from its plane: this sets how wide the return current spreads under it (about 1 / (1 + (x/h)²)) and the loop inductance, the figure that matters most.
  • εr and loss tangent: not the current paths or the loop inductance (the magnetics depend on the geometry only), but the planes' capacitance to each other (ε0·εr·A/t: the return crossing between planes, plane resonances, damped by the loss tangent) and the line itself (its Z0 and speed from a cross-section with the real dielectrics and solder mask: the line current at each harmonic, the delay). One εr per dielectric, not frequency dependent.

Victims: how they are found, how far to trust them. No full-wave simulation: each switching leg's hot loop is solved once more on a model that also holds the victims (their tracks and pads, the victim ICs' ground pins, a stacked board's connector pins), at two frequencies of the edge's spectrum. Each victim net is copper in that model, driven at its driver through an output resistance (a pull-up's value, else victims.drive_ohms, 50 Ω) and loaded at each receiver by victims.input_pf (5 pF) to that receiver's own ground. The solution gives the voltage across each receiver's input directly: the hot loop's field in the victim's loop and the ground moving between driver and receiver together (peak at an edge M·I/t_r). A victim track facing a phase node with no layer between takes C·V/t_r through that capacitance. This works because at switching edges the board is small against the wavelength (a 5 ns edge: mostly below ~70 MHz, a wavelength of about 2 m in FR-4), where coupling is magnetic and capacitive, which the quasi-static model holds.

  • Good for: finding and ranking the nets at risk, and comparing fixes (moving a reset track, a ground connection, a stitching via): the differences are reliable.
  • Absolute values: expect a factor of about 2 (±6 dB) at best. They hang on the terminations (the 50 Ω and 5 pF defaults; a real input, ESD diodes or a filter capacitor change them: set drive_ohms / input_pf), on two frequencies only (ringing elsewhere can be missed), on no cable or common-mode currents (often the real coupling path), and above roughly 100–300 MHz (fast GaN edges, long tracks) on the quasi-static limit.
  • No upset prediction: the warning levels (0.3 V noise, 1 mA injected) are rules of thumb. For a marginal case, measure: a near-field probe, or the reset line on a scope while the bridge switches.
